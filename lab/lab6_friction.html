1 Ride the Physics Core Values Cart around a corner

Go outside or down the hall and have fun trying to push each other around a corner while riding the Physics Core Values Cart.

  • Where: outside is best, at the 5-point intersection west of the Wyse entrance
  • Group: at least two
Servant Leader1 person pushes the cart
Passionate Learner2 people ride on the cart
Compassionate Peacemakerseveryone else stands outside the end of the curve to stop the cart from crashing

Name the four core values surrounding “Christ-centered” (Passionate Learners, Servant Leaders, Compassionate Peacemakers, Global Citizens).

Procedure

  1. Start the cart 10 yards back on the sidewalk toward the Union and come toward Wyse at a very brisk walking pace.
  2. Only pushing, no pulling, but you may push in any direction on any side of the cart, or against the rider’s thighs or knees.
  3. Guide the cart around the corner so it heads toward Umble.
  4. After a try, ask among yourselves: What are you trying to do, and how are you trying to push? What direction are you pushing? Did you ever change direction? Should you?
  5. Rotate after each try or two, so each person gets a chance and each contributes to the solution.
  6. Once you succeed with one giant push, try to follow the curve more closely, guiding the cart slowly all the way around. Don’t slow down! Observe the direction in which you push the cart.

Think about the velocity at each point and how it is changing. Lay out the vector arrows for the direction of velocity at each point. What direction is the acceleration? What direction must the net force be?

2 Rolling and sliding friction

  • 2 m inclined track
  • Cart + added weight
  • Green motion sensor, ball sensitivity, at the lower end
  • LabPro + Logger Pro
  • 40 samples/s, 25 s

Part A: free rolling

Set the sample rate and duration with the button just left of the green one. Start data collection and give the cart a gentle shove up the track, starting it more than 15 cm (the sensor’s minimum distance) from the sensor, and get your hand out of the way quickly. Adjust the scales so you can see the details of the free-rolling part of the position and velocity graphs.

These graphs should remind you of a ball tossed up; be sure you understand them. What is the acceleration of the cart? Find it crudely by looking at one of the graphs, then get a precise value using the features of Logger Pro.

Part B: added sliding friction

Turn the little brass knob on the cart to add some sliding friction, but not so much that the cart sticks in one place. Repeat the data collection. Do you see evidence of this friction in your data? For this case:

  • Draw free-body diagrams of the cart on the way up and on the way down. You may exaggerate the tilt of the track.
  • Choose a convenient coordinate system.
  • Relate the forces on your diagrams to the cart’s acceleration using Newton’s 2nd law, drawing the arrows to scale.
  • Check carefully: if there is no acceleration there should be no net force; if there is acceleration, the net force should point along it.
  • Notice how the two diagrams differ. Can you explain the two different slopes in the velocity plot using the two free-body diagrams?

Part C: pure rolling

Turn the knob to retract the friction pad completely and repeat. Now that you know what to look for, do you see any evidence of pure rolling friction? The carts are good but not perfect. A straight edge on the screen, or sighting down the line from the side, helps you check whether the velocity plot is really straight.

Derivation: acceleration with friction, going up and coming down

Use your free-body diagrams from Part B. Let the track make angle θ with the horizontal, let the cart have mass m, and model the friction pad with kinetic coefficient μk. Take +x to point up the incline.

  1. Write Newton’s 2nd law perpendicular to the track and find the normal force N.
  2. Write Newton’s 2nd law along the track for the cart moving up, and solve explicitly for aup in terms of g, θ, and μk.
  3. Repeat for the cart moving down, and solve explicitly for adown. Explain why the friction term changes sign but the gravity term does not.
  4. Which has the larger magnitude? Does this agree with the two slopes in your velocity plot? What happens to your two expressions when μk = 0?
  5. Using your two measured slopes, combine your expressions to find numerical values for μk and θ. Check θ against a quick measurement of the track’s rise and length.

3 Static and kinetic friction

  • Dual-Range Force Sensor
  • LabPro + Logger Pro
  • Wood block + 2 kg mass
  • Digital scale

Set up a single graph of sensor force (y-axis) versus time (x-axis). Start data collection and use the force sensor to pull the wood block with 2 kg on top. Pull very gradually harder and harder until it begins to move, then keep pulling slowly and steadily for a few seconds, then stop. Repeat the whole process several times. Your force vs. time graph should look roughly like this:

Note the following features

  • Static friction takes on a wide range of values, from zero up to some limit. Do you see why the equation for static friction has an inequality?
  • Kinetic friction is relatively constant. Some variation in the plotted value while the block slides does not necessarily mean the friction is varying. What else could it be? Can you think of a way of pulling that would cause a variation? How can you minimize it?
  • Can you show that kinetic friction does not depend on speed? How can you do this given the problem you just noted? Think about this carefully.
  • There is sometimes a visible difference between the upper limit of static friction and the constant value of kinetic friction. What does this mean about the two coefficients?
  • How should this graph change if you add mass? Do it and see.
  • How should it change if you change the surface being tested? Try it.

Now be quantitative

You have several materials that you can use in different combinations. Measure the coefficient of kinetic friction for different objects on the table surface.

  1. Weigh the object without the 2 kg on the digital scale, then add the 2 kg and determine the normal force N.
  2. Use your skill to pull at constant speed, and measure the kinetic friction force from the Logger Pro data.
  3. Use the friction equation fk = μkN to calculate the coefficient of kinetic friction.
  4. Repeat to determine the coefficient for a total of four different combinations of materials.

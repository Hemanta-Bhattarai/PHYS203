How to use this lab: in each experiment, answer the 💡 Brainstorm questions first — talk with your group and write down your predictions before you try it. Then use the 🧠 Discussion questions while you work and afterward to explain what you saw.

1Air Track Dialog

Go to the front bench for a dialog on the big air track with your instructor. Look at the motion of the cart and notice how it differs from ordinary things.

Try it:

  1. Make the cart move faster and faster. Should you push harder and harder, or push with a steady force? Try both.
  2. Look for a threshold: push with a steady force small enough that you see no effect on the air track. Then take the cart, set it on the table, and compare.
  3. Your instructor starts the cart; you exert the force needed to keep it moving at constant speed.
  4. Instructor demo: a large force is applied, then slowly backed off. Watch what the velocity does while the force is backed off.
  5. Slow the cart down steadily. Then try: (A) slow down, (B) pass through zero velocity, and (C) speed up the other way.
Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. On an ordinary table you have to keep pushing to keep a cart moving. If you give the air-track cart one push and let go, what do you predict it will do? Why?
  2. To make the cart go faster and faster, do you expect to need a steadily increasing push or a steady push? Predict, then try.
  3. What do you think the air is doing? What has it taken away compared with the table?
Discussion
  1. How does the motion of the air-track cart differ from ordinary things?
  2. Is there a threshold — a force so small that you see no effect? What changes when you repeat it on the table?
  3. What is the “appropriate” force to keep the cart moving at constant speed? Explain using force and velocity.
  4. While the large force is slowly backed off, what is the velocity doing — speeding up, slowing down, staying the same, or something else?
  5. When you slow the cart down steadily, which way is the acceleration? Why? (Think about the force and the velocity.)
  6. Slow down (A), pass through zero (B), speed up the other way (C): which way is the acceleration at each stage? Does it ever change direction? Why?

2Ride and Push the GC Physics Phlyer

Use the Dynamic Display Device connected to the Physics Phlyer. Turn it on. It should do the following:

  1. Show a hyphen for about 5 seconds (if it is stuck here, reboot).
  2. Say some stuff, then display the battery voltages. They should be 6.5 V or more.
  3. Zero the force plate — do not touch the force handles at this time.
  4. Come to the menu with the main program ready. Press GO to start the program.

One of you rides the cart while another pushes. Switch roles so everyone does both.

aLearning what it does

Have one of you ride the cart while another pushes it back and forth a bit, and see what this amazing device can show you. Watch the force display as you push and pull, the velocity display as you move fast and slow forward and backward, and the acceleration display.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. Which display do you expect to change first when you start pushing — force, acceleration, or velocity?
  2. When the cart moves at a steady speed, what do you expect the acceleration display to show?
Discussion
  1. Can you see how the acceleration display is related to the velocity display? Explain how.
  2. Look at the signs. Can you get positive velocity with negative acceleration? Can you have negative velocity with positive acceleration? Prove it (describe the motion that does it).

bConstant force

With one person riding on the cart and one pushing, pick some constant level on the force display (not very large) and try to push with that constant force as long as possible. This is very hard to do — try again to do a better job. The person pushing should concentrate on the force graph and try to keep the force constant. The others should watch the other bar graphs.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. If you push with a constant force, what will the velocity do? What will the acceleration do? Write your predictions down before you try.
Discussion
  1. What happens to your velocity when you push with a constant force?
  2. Is the velocity staying constant as you push with a constant force? Should it? Does it reach a maximum, or does it seem like it could keep climbing?
  3. Did the person pushing notice that you have to work harder and harder to keep pushing with a constant force? Why?
  4. What is the acceleration doing? Is it approximately constant? Do it again and watch the acceleration graph.

cCoast

Give a big shove and then let go and allow the cart to coast. Now you are not applying any force, but there is still just a bit of friction.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. If there were truly no friction, what would you predict the velocity does after you let go? And the acceleration?
Discussion
  1. Look at the velocity indicator. Can you imagine what it would be doing if there were truly no friction?
  2. Look at the acceleration display. Do you see a hint of acceleration? Which way does it point, and why?

dPush, coast, pull

Push steadily for a bit, then let it coast for a bit, then pull steadily to bring the cart to a stop.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. Before you do it, sketch your predicted force, velocity, and acceleration versus time for the push–coast–pull motion (three graphs sharing one time axis).
Discussion
  1. What is each indicator doing during each part of the motion?
  2. Does the force seem to match velocity or acceleration? Which one? Does this make sense? Says who? Can you relate this to some fundamental law?
  3. How do the displays compare with the graphs you sketched? What did you get right or wrong?

ePush crazy

Push in mixed-up ways while you watch the display.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. Do you expect force and acceleration always to have the same sign? Always about the same size?
Discussion
  1. Does force always match acceleration?
  2. Do the signs always agree?
  3. Pay close attention to the magnitudes: do force and acceleration have about the same value, or is one larger than the other?

fEmpty cart

Try pushing and pulling with no one on the cart.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. For the same push, will the acceleration be larger, smaller, or the same without the rider? Why?
Discussion
  1. Now look at the relationship between force and acceleration. Has it changed?
  2. If the cart by itself has about the same mass as a person, does this new relationship between force and acceleration make sense?

gTwo on the cart

Have two people ride the cart and push and pull as you watch the displays.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. With two riders and the same force, what do you predict will happen to the acceleration? How would you describe what “mass” does?
Discussion
  1. What is the relationship now between force and acceleration? Does this again make sense?
  2. Do you begin to understand what mass is? State your understanding in a sentence or two.

3Force Plate Experiments

Set-up of program and sensors

  1. Start up Logger Pro with the force plate and acceleration sensors connected. The default graphs are just fine. Or, for better graphs, set the sample rate to 200 per second and the duration to 10 seconds; graph Force vs. Time with scale from −1000 to 3000 N and Acceleration vs. Time with scale from −30 to +30 m/s2. Close the chart and all the other windows.
  2. Zero both sensors. Set the accelerometer on the bench with its arrow pointing up, stay off the force plate, then click the “Zero” button. Check both sensor boxes to get them both zeroed.

1Jumping on the force plate while holding the accelerometer

Have one person hold the accelerometer on top of their head or against their chest, keeping the arrow pointing up. Click Collect, then have the person step on the force plate, then jump, land, and stand still for a bit before jumping again — perhaps higher, or lower, or more slowly…

Now look at your graphs and try to understand most or all of the details you see. Don’t settle for “here’s the jump” — find each part of the jump and think about what is happening.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. What number do you expect the force plate to read while you stand still? What do you expect it to read while you are in the air?
  2. Sketch your predicted force and acceleration graphs for: stand still → crouch → push off → in the air → land → stand still.
  3. While you are in the air, what acceleration do you expect? Is there a special value?
Discussion
  1. Look for a special value that makes sense on the acceleration plot. For what period of time does it have that value? During that time period, what is the force?
  2. Look at the beginning of the jump. Notice what precedes the actual jump. When are the knees bent the most? Are you sure you are distinguishing between acceleration, velocity, and position?
  3. When are the feet pressing hardest? Are the knees bent at that moment?
  4. What part of the action does the dip in force really represent? Do all this in as much detail as possible.

2Tossing a heavy ball while standing on the force plate

Set the accelerometer aside and have the heavy ball ready. Start data collection. One person steps on the force plate and stands still. Hand the heavy ball to that person. Then, while standing on the plate, they throw the ball straight up and catch it again.

Look at your graphs and try to understand most or all of the details you see. Again, look in detail.

Brainstorm — predict before you try
  1. How will the force reading while you hold the ball compare to standing without it? By how much do you expect it to change?
  2. While the ball is in the air, will the plate read more than, less than, or the same as when you stood before the ball was handed to you? Why?
  3. What do you expect the plate to do at the instant of the throw? At the instant of the catch?
Discussion
  1. Identify each of these periods on your graph: stepping on the scale, standing still, holding the ball, throwing the ball, ball in the air, catching the ball, holding it again.
  2. Compare “standing before the ball” with “ball is in the air” by finding the average over the cleanest part of each region. Do they agree? Do you see why they should?
  3. Can you give a clear explanation of why the act of throwing the ball increases the force on the floor?

✓Putting It Together

Discussion — across all stations
  1. Using what you saw at all three stations, how does an object’s acceleration depend on the force applied and on its mass? State it in your own words, then as an equation.
  2. Does a force determine an object’s velocity or its acceleration? What evidence from this lab supports your answer?
  3. While you are in the air during a jump, the force plate reads zero, but your acceleration is not zero. Why doesn’t this contradict Newton’s second law? Which force(s) act on you?

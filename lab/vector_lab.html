Triangle Law 15 min

Definition If two vectors A and B are arranged so that the tail of B touches the head of A, then their sum R = A + B is the vector drawn from the tail of A to the head of B — the third side of the triangle formed.
Worked example A = (4, 2) is drawn from the origin. B = (1, 3) is drawn starting at A's head, i.e., from (4,2) to (4+1, 2+3) = (5,5). The resultant R runs from the original tail (the origin) to B's final head: R = (5, 5) = A + B.

Parallelogram Law 15 min

Definition If two vectors A and B are drawn tail-to-tail from a common origin, and a parallelogram is completed using them as adjacent sides, then their sum R = A + B is the diagonal of that parallelogram, starting at the common origin.
Worked example The same A = (4, 2) and B = (1, 3) are drawn from the same origin this time (not head-to-tail). Dashed lines complete the parallelogram, and the diagonal from the origin gives R = (5, 5) — exactly the same resultant as the Triangle Law found.

Vector Subtraction 12 min

Definition A − B is defined as A + (−B), where −B has the same magnitude as B but points in the exact opposite direction (rotated 180°). Once −B is drawn, the subtraction is just a Triangle Law addition of A and −B.
Worked example A = (4, 3), B = (2, 4). Reversing B gives −B = (−2, −4). Applying the Triangle Law to A and −B (placing −B's tail at A's head) gives D = A − B = (2, −1).

Polygon Law 12 min

Definition The Triangle Law extends to any number of vectors: place each vector's tail at the previous vector's head, in sequence. The resultant R is drawn from the very first tail to the very last head, closing the polygon.
Worked example A = (3, 1), B = (1, 3), C = (−2, −1), chained head-to-tail in sequence. The resultant R runs from the first tail (origin) to C's final head: R = (2, 3) = A + B + C.

Scalar Multiplication 12 min

Definition Multiplying a vector A by a scalar k produces kA: a vector |k| times as long as A, pointing in the same direction if k > 0, or the exact opposite direction if k < 0.
Worked example A = (2, 3). With k = −1.5, kA = (−1.5×2, −1.5×3) = (−3, −4.5): 1.5 times as long as A, pointing the opposite way since k is negative.

i-j Verification & Wrap-Up 16 min

Every vector you drew can also be written in i-j (component) form — for example, A = 3i + 2j means A has an x-component of 3 and a y-component of 2. This is the algebraic version of everything you just did geometrically: add components separately to add vectors, and multiply each component to scale a vector.

Task

Go back to every problem in every section above (all 20). For each one, write every given vector in i-j form, then add (or subtract, or scale) the i-components together and the j-components together by hand, showing your work. Compare this i-j result to the components you read off the grid geometrically. Do they match exactly? If not, go back and re-check your construction.

In one or two sentences: which method (Triangle, Parallelogram, Polygon, or i-j components) would you reach for first if you needed to add five vectors quickly and accurately? Why?

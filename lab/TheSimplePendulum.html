The Simple Pendulum

A mass on a string, swinging under gravity. You will predict how its period depends on length using only units, measure that dependence, extract a power law from a log‑log plot, and use it to measure the acceleration due to gravity here in Goshen.

Goals & equipment Pre‑lab Procedure Task 1: Data Task 2: Logger Pro Task 3: Prediction Task 4: Finding g Discussion Brainstorm

Goals and equipment

By the end of this lab you will be able to:

  1. Use dimensional analysis to predict how a physical quantity scales with the variables it depends on.
  2. Reduce timing error by measuring many oscillations and averaging repeated trials.
  3. Linearize a power law with a log‑log plot and extract the exponent and prefactor, with uncertainties, from a fit.
  4. Compare an experimental value of \(g\) with an accepted value and reason about random and systematic error.

Pre‑lab: dimensional analysis

Complete before you touch the pendulum.

A simple pendulum is a point mass hung from a fixed point by a massless string. At equilibrium the string hangs vertically. Displace the bob slightly and it oscillates back and forth about equilibrium.

The period might depend on the mass of the bob \(m\), the length of the string \(L\), and the acceleration due to gravity \(g\). Suppose

\[ T = A\, m^{x}\, L^{y}\, g^{z}, \]

where \(A\) is a dimensionless constant. Write the dimensions of each side in terms of mass [M], length [L], and time [T], and require them to match.

Procedure: measuring the period

  1. Set the string length \(L\), measured from the pivot to the center of the bob.
  2. Pull the bob aside with the string taut, keeping the angle from vertical under \(10^\circ\). Check with the protractor.
  3. Release the bob without pushing it. Let it complete one or two swings before you start timing.
  4. One period \(T\) is one complete back‑and‑forth swing. Time 10 complete oscillations with your phone stopwatch and record that time \(t_{10}\).
  5. Repeat the 10‑oscillation measurement 5 times for the same length.
  6. Each trial gives \(T = t_{10}/10\). Average the 5 values to get the period for that length.
  7. Repeat for all 5 lengths.
Tip. Start and stop the timer as the bob passes through the lowest point, where it moves fastest. Count “zero” at the start, not “one.”

Task 2: Finding the power law in Logger Pro

  1. Open Logger Pro. Double‑click the “X” column header in the data set. Set the name to Length, short name L, units m.
  2. Double‑click the “Y” column header. Set the name to Time period, short name T, units s.
  3. Enter your lengths and average periods from Task 1.
  4. Look at the plot of \(T\) against \(L\). Does the period increase with length? Is the trend a straight line? It is hard to judge on linear axes.
  5. Click the y‑axis label, choose More, check Log Axis, and click OK.
  6. Click the x‑axis label, choose More, check Log Axis, and click OK.
  7. Do the points now fall on a straight line? A power law \(T = A L^{B}\) becomes a straight line on a log‑log plot because \(\ln T = \ln A + B \ln L\). The slope is \(B\).
  8. Drag to select all points. Choose Analyze → Curve Fit, select Power as the general equation, and fit. Record:

Task 3: Prediction

  1. Choose a length that is not in your table. In Logger Pro, use your power fit (or Analyze → Interpolate) to predict its period.
  2. Set the pendulum to that length and measure the period using the same procedure.
  3. Do the prediction and the measurement agree within uncertainty?

Task 4: Measuring g

Later in the course we will derive, for small angles,

\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}. \]
  1. Compare this equation with your fitted form \(T = A L^{B}\). What are \(B\) and \(A\) in terms of \(g\)?
  2. Does the theoretical \(B\) fall inside your interval \((B - \Delta B,\ B + \Delta B)\)? If not, what could explain the discrepancy?
  3. Use your fitted \(A\) to solve for \(g\). Propagate the uncertainty with \[ \Delta g = 2\,\frac{\Delta A}{A}\, g. \] Report the interval \((g - \Delta g,\ g + \Delta g)\).
  4. The accepted local value in Goshen is \(g = 9.8022\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). Does it fall inside your interval? How well did you do?

Discussion questions

Answer these in your lab report. Use your own data and numbers wherever you can.

  1. Dimensional analysis gave you the exponents but not \(A\). Explain why mass cannot appear in the result. What physical reasoning, beyond units, also tells you the period should not depend on mass?theory
  2. Human reaction time with a stopwatch is about \(0.1\)–\(0.2\ \mathrm{s}\). Estimate the fractional error in \(T\) if you timed one oscillation versus ten. Why does timing many oscillations help, and is there a limit to how many you should time?uncertainty
  3. Why should the amplitude stay below \(10^\circ\)? The large‑amplitude period is approximately \(T \approx T_0\left(1 + \theta_0^2/16\right)\) with \(\theta_0\) in radians. Compute the fractional change at \(10^\circ\) and at \(30^\circ\).model limits
  4. What exactly is “\(L\)”? If you had measured to the top of the bob instead of its center, how would \(A\), \(B\), and your value of \(g\) change? Is this a random or a systematic error?systematics
  5. Explain in your own words why a power law appears as a straight line on a log‑log plot. What would an exponential relationship look like on the same axes?analysis
  6. Is it better to start and stop the timer at the lowest point of the swing or at a turning point? Justify your answer using the bob’s speed at each location.technique

Brainstorming questions

Open questions with no single right answer. Pick one or two, discuss them with your group, and sketch an experiment you could actually run.

  1. Design a clean test of whether the period depends on mass. What would you hold fixed, what would you vary, and how would you keep the effective length constant when bobs have different sizes?
  2. Your phone has an accelerometer and a gyroscope. How could you hang the phone itself as the bob and measure \(T\) without a stopwatch? (Look up the free app phyphox.) What else could you learn from the full time series, such as how quickly the swing dies out?
  3. Record a video of the pendulum and track the bob frame by frame (for example with the free program Tracker). How could you test the amplitude correction from discussion question 3 and find the angle where the small‑angle formula fails?
  4. Historically, pendulums kept time. What length gives a period of exactly \(2\ \mathrm{s}\) (a “seconds pendulum,” one second per swing)? Predict it, build it, and test it.
  5. What would happen to the period if the pendulum were in an elevator accelerating upward, or on a cart accelerating sideways? How about on the Moon or Mars?

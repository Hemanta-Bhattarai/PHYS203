Chapter 7: Work and Energy

Class Day 19: Work and the Work-Energy Theorem

Why this matters

Energy methods offer yet another powerful alternative to F=ma, especially useful when forces vary over a distance or when you care about speed at a location rather than the full time-history of the motion. Today builds the foundational definitions: work and kinetic energy.

Defining work

Work done by a constant force is:

\[W = Fd\cos\theta\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the force direction and the direction of displacement. The \(\cos\theta\) factor captures an important and often counterintuitive idea: only the component of force along the direction of motion does any work at all. A force applied exactly perpendicular to an object’s displacement ((°), so \(\cos\theta\)) does zero work, no matter how large that force is. This is why, technically, carrying a heavy bag at constant height across a flat floor involves no work being done against gravity by your arm — your upward-supporting force is perpendicular to your horizontal motion.

Work can also be negative: if the force has a component opposing the direction of motion ((>90°)), \(\cos\theta\) is negative, and the work done is negative — this happens whenever a force is slowing something down, like friction or air resistance acting against a moving object.

For a variable force (one that changes over the distance traveled), work is found as the area under a force-vs-displacement graph — this generalizes the constant-force formula (which is just the area of a simple rectangle, (Fd)) to any shape of force curve.

Kinetic energy and the work-energy theorem

Kinetic energy is defined as:

\[KE = \tfrac12mv^2\]

The work-energy theorem connects work directly to a change in kinetic energy:

\[W_{net} = \Delta KE = KE_f - KE_i\]

This is often dramatically faster than a kinematics-based approach when you only care about speed at a certain point, not the full time history of the motion.

Worked example: A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest to 25 m/s. Find its final kinetic energy and the net work done on it.

\[KE_f = \tfrac12(1200)(25)^2 = 375{,}000\text{ J}\]

Since \(KE_i=0\) (starts from rest), the work-energy theorem gives \(W_{net}=\Delta KE = 375{,}000-0=375{,}000\) J directly — no need to know the acceleration, the distance, or the time separately; the net work is just the final kinetic energy.

Common mistake to watch for

Using the work done by only one force (say, an applied push) in the work-energy theorem, while ignoring other forces (like friction) also acting on the object. The theorem requires the net work — the work done by the combination of every force acting on the object — not the work done by any single force in isolation, unless that force genuinely is the only one present.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Work by a constant force: (W=Fd); a force perpendicular to displacement does zero work; a force opposing motion does negative work.
  • Variable-force work = area under a force-vs-displacement graph.
  • Kinetic energy: \(KE=\tfrac12mv^2\). Work-energy theorem: \(W_{net}=\Delta KE\).

Class Day 20: Potential Energy and Conservation of Energy

Why this matters

Potential energy captures “stored” energy tied to an object’s position or configuration, and today’s conservation-of-energy principle becomes one of the single most powerful problem-solving tools in the entire course — often replacing several steps of kinematics with one clean equation.

Two kinds of potential energy for this course

Gravitational potential energy, \(PE_{grav}=mgh\), where (h) is height measured relative to some chosen reference level. The choice of reference level is arbitrary — what actually matters physically is always the change in height (and therefore the change in PE), never some absolute value tied to an arbitrarily chosen “zero” point.

Elastic potential energy, stored in a stretched or compressed spring: \(PE_{elastic}=\tfrac12kx^2\), where (k) is the spring constant and (x) is the displacement from the spring’s natural (unstretched) length. This connects back to yesterday’s area-under-the-curve idea: a spring’s restoring force grows linearly with stretch ((F=kx), Hooke’s law), so the work needed to stretch it is the area of a triangle under that linearly rising force-vs-displacement graph, which works out to exactly \(\tfrac12kx^2\).

Conservation of mechanical energy

In the absence of non-conservative forces (like friction or air resistance), the total mechanical energy — kinetic plus potential — remains constant throughout a motion:

\[KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f\]

Worked example: A 5 kg object slides down a frictionless 15 m tall hill, starting from rest. Find its speed at the bottom.

Taking the bottom of the hill as the reference height (\(PE_f=0\)):

\[0 + mgh = \tfrac12mv_f^2 + 0 \Rightarrow v_f = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(15)} = 17.1\text{ m/s}\]

Notice mass cancels out entirely — just as with the frictionless incline in Chapter 5, the final speed at the bottom of a frictionless hill doesn’t depend on how heavy the object is, only on the height dropped. This is really the same underlying physics as free fall and frictionless inclines, viewed through a different (and often more convenient) lens.

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting to account for all forms of energy present in a given problem — a common one is a spring launching a ball vertically, where both elastic PE (at the start) and gravitational PE (at the peak) matter, and skipping either term breaks the conservation equation. Before writing the energy equation, explicitly list every energy type present at the initial state and every energy type present at the final state.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Gravitational PE: (mgh) (only changes in height matter physically). Elastic PE: \(\tfrac12kx^2\).
  • Conservation of mechanical energy: \(KE_i+PE_i = KE_f+PE_f\), valid when only conservative forces act.
  • Before applying the energy equation, list every energy type present at both the initial and final states.

Class Day 21: Applications of Conservation of Energy

Why this matters

Today is a fluency day: taking yesterday’s conservation principle and applying it to richer, multi-stage, real-world-style scenarios — including situations where friction or other non-conservative forces are also present.

Extending the equation to include energy losses

When friction or another non-conservative force is present, mechanical energy is no longer perfectly conserved — some is lost to that other process (usually converted to heat). The conservation equation is extended to explicitly account for this:

\[KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + |E_{lost}|\]

The lost energy term is simply added to whichever side represents “later” in the process, since that energy has left the mechanical (KE + PE) system entirely.

Worked example: A roller coaster car (480 kg) at the top of a 30 m hill (starting from rest) encounters friction doing −20,000 J of work by the bottom. Find its speed at the bottom.

\[mgh = \tfrac12mv_f^2 + |E_{lost}|\] \[480(9.8)(30) = \tfrac12(480)v_f^2 + 20{,}000\] \[141{,}120 = 240v_f^2 + 20{,}000 \Rightarrow v_f^2 = \frac{121{,}120}{240} = 504.7 \Rightarrow v_f = 22.5\text{ m/s}\]

Segmenting multi-stage problems

A common and important problem type: an object slides down a frictionless section, then continues onto a section with friction. The clean way to handle this is to treat it in two stages: first, apply pure conservation of energy across the frictionless section to find the speed entering the second section; then, apply the work-energy theorem (or the extended energy equation with a loss term) across the frictional section separately, using that speed as the new starting point.

Worked example (segmented problem): A 60 kg skier starts from rest at the top of a 45 m frictionless slope, then continues onto a horizontal section with friction (\(\mu_k=0.1\)). Find the skier’s speed at the bottom of the slope, then find how far they travel on the horizontal section before stopping.

Stage 1 (frictionless slope): (v = = = 29.7) m/s.

Stage 2 (horizontal, with friction): all of the kinetic energy at the bottom must be dissipated by friction to bring the skier to rest. Using the work-energy theorem, (-fd = 0 - KE), where \(f=\mu_kmg\):

\[\mu_kmg\cdot d = \tfrac12mv^2 \Rightarrow d = \frac{v^2}{2\mu_kg} = \frac{29.7^2}{2(0.1)(9.8)} = 450\text{ m}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Trying to apply a single conservation-of-energy equation across an entire multi-stage journey that mixes frictionless and frictional sections, without separating them. The energy-loss term only applies where friction (or another non-conservative force) is actually acting — bundling everything into one equation without correctly bookkeeping where the loss occurs is a common source of error in these richer problems.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • With non-conservative losses: \(KE_i+PE_i =\ KE_f+PE_f+|E_{lost}|\).
  • Multi-stage problems (frictionless section followed by a frictional section) are best solved by treating each section separately, using the end-of-stage-one speed as the start of stage two.
  • Segment any problem at the exact point where the physical situation (friction on/off, spring engaged/released, etc.) changes.

Chapter 9: Oscillations

Class Day 28: Simple Harmonic Motion I

Why this matters

So far, every motion we’ve studied has either continued in one direction or eventually stopped. Oscillation introduces something new: motion that repeats itself indefinitely, back and forth around a central equilibrium point. Simple harmonic motion (SHM) is the cleanest, most important version of this, and it shows up throughout physics, from springs to pendulums to the vibration of atoms in a solid.

What makes motion “simple harmonic”

SHM is defined by one specific condition on the restoring force: it must be directly proportional to displacement from equilibrium, and always point back toward equilibrium:

\[F = -kx\]

This is Hooke’s law from your work with springs, generalized as the defining feature of the entire category of motion. The negative sign is the whole story: whenever the object is displaced in either direction, the force always points back the other way, continually pulling it back toward center — which is exactly what produces a repeating, oscillating motion rather than the object simply flying off or settling into a new resting position.

Core vocabulary

  • Amplitude ((A)): the maximum displacement from equilibrium — how far the motion swings out on either side.
  • Period ((T)): the time for one complete cycle (out and back to the starting point and direction).
  • Frequency ((f)): the number of complete cycles per second, related to period by (f=1/T).
  • Phase: describes where in the cycle the oscillator is at a given instant — useful for comparing two oscillators of the same frequency that are offset in time from each other (out of sync, or “out of phase”).

Worked example: An oscillator completes 12 cycles in 3 s. Find its period and frequency.

\[f = \frac{12\text{ cycles}}{3\text{ s}} = 4\text{ Hz} \qquad T = \frac{1}{f} = 0.25\text{ s}\]

The deep connection to circular motion

SHM can be understood as the “shadow” of uniform circular motion — imagine an object moving in a circle at constant speed, and watch only its projection onto one axis (say, just the horizontal shadow it casts). That shadow moves back and forth exactly like SHM, speeding up through the center and slowing to a stop at the extremes, which is precisely why sine and cosine functions (themselves defined via circular motion) are the natural mathematical language for describing SHM position over time.

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming a larger-amplitude oscillation must have a longer period, reasoning that it “has farther to travel.” For true SHM, this is false — period depends only on the physical properties of the system (as the next class day will make precise for springs and pendulums), not on amplitude at all. A pendulum swinging through a wide arc and the same pendulum swinging through a narrow arc complete each cycle in the same amount of time (as long as the small-angle approximation holds).

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • SHM requires a restoring force proportional to displacement: (F=-kx).
  • Amplitude (max displacement), period (time per cycle), frequency ((f=1/T), cycles per second), and phase (position within the cycle) are the core descriptive vocabulary.
  • SHM is the one-dimensional projection (“shadow”) of uniform circular motion.

Class Day 29: Simple Harmonic Motion II (Mass-Spring Systems and Pendulums)

Why this matters

Today applies yesterday’s general SHM framework to the two most common physical examples in this course: a mass on a spring, and a simple pendulum. Both turn out to have clean formulas for period that depend only on the system’s physical properties, never on amplitude.

Mass-spring systems

For a mass (m) attached to a spring with spring constant (k) (recall (F=-kx) from Hooke’s law), the period of oscillation is:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\]

The single most important feature of this formula, worth dwelling on: period does not depend on amplitude at all — only on the mass and the spring constant. Pull a mass on a spring back a little, or a lot, and it completes each full oscillation in exactly the same time either way (a larger pull just means the mass also moves faster at each point, exactly balancing out the longer distance it needs to cover).

Worked example: A 3 kg mass oscillates on a spring with (k=300) N/m. Find the period and frequency.

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3}{300}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.01} = 2\pi(0.1) = 0.628\text{ s} \qquad f = \frac{1}{0.628} = 1.59\text{ Hz}\]

Simple pendulums

For a simple pendulum of length (L) swinging under gravity (g), the period is:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\]

— valid only under the small-angle approximation (roughly, swings of less than about 15°-20°). Two features are worth emphasizing: period depends on length and on local gravity, but not on the mass of the bob at all, and (like the spring) not on amplitude either, as long as the angle stays small. This mirrors free fall from Chapter 2, where all masses accelerate identically under gravity — here too, a heavier or lighter pendulum bob swings with exactly the same period, because the same gravitational acceleration acts on both, regardless of mass.

Worked example (the “seconds pendulum”): A pendulum clock is designed to have a period of exactly 2 s. Find the required length.

\[2 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{9.8}} \Rightarrow 1 = \pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{9.8}} \Rightarrow \frac{L}{9.8} = \frac{1}{\pi^2} \Rightarrow L = \frac{9.8}{\pi^2} = 0.993\text{ m}\]

This is a genuinely useful, real-world result: a pendulum roughly 1 meter long has a period of very close to 2 seconds, which is why old pendulum clocks are typically built to about that size. Notice also that if this clock were moved somewhere with different local gravity (say, a different altitude, or a different planet), the same physical pendulum length would produce a different period, since (g) appears directly in the formula — a pendulum clock accurate on Earth would run at the wrong rate on the Moon, without adjusting its length to compensate.

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming the mass-dependence rule for pendulums (period independent of mass) also applies for a different, unstated reason than gravity’s mass-independence — the two formulas (spring vs. pendulum) arrive at “period doesn’t depend on some variable” through genuinely different physical mechanisms (the spring formula does depend on mass; the pendulum formula does not, precisely because gravity’s acceleration doesn’t depend on mass while a spring’s restoring force per unit mass does). Don’t over-generalize one system’s independence rule onto the other without checking the actual formula.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Mass-spring period: (T=2) — independent of amplitude, but does depend on mass.
  • Simple pendulum period: (T=2) — independent of both amplitude (small-angle) and mass, but depends on local (g).
  • Both formulas are valid only under their respective assumptions (Hooke’s law holding for the spring; small-angle approximation for the pendulum).

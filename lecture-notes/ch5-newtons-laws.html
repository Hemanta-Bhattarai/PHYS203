Chapter 5: Newton’s Laws and Applications

Class Day 13: Newton’s Laws and Different Forces

Why this matters

Everything up to this point has been about describing motion. Starting today, we ask the deeper question: why does an object move the way it does? Newton’s three laws are the answer, and F = ma (the second law) becomes the single most-used equation for the rest of the course.

The first law: inertia

An object remains at rest, or moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted on by a net (unbalanced) force. The word “net” is doing a lot of work here — an object can have several forces acting on it simultaneously and still not accelerate at all, as long as those forces perfectly balance. A book sitting on a table has both gravity and the table’s normal force acting on it; since these are equal and opposite, the net force is zero, and the book stays put, consistent with the first law.

The counterintuitive part for most students is the “moving at constant velocity” half of the law. Everyday experience (friction, air resistance) makes it seem like objects naturally slow down and require continuous force just to keep moving — but that’s the effect of friction, an actual force, not a natural tendency of motion itself. On a genuinely frictionless surface, a puck given a single push would continue gliding forever at that same constant velocity, needing no ongoing force at all.

The second law: F = ma

The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration:

\[\vec F_{net} = m\vec a\]

This is the central computational engine of the rest of the course. The practical workflow is always the same: identify every force acting on an object, draw a free-body diagram (a simple sketch showing the object as a point or box with an arrow for each force acting on it), sum the forces (as vectors — this is why Chapter 3 mattered), and set that sum equal to (ma).

Worked example: A 5 kg object experiences a net force of 15 N. Find its acceleration.

\[a = \frac{F_{net}}{m} = \frac{15}{5} = 3\text{ m/s}^2\]

The third law: action-reaction pairs

For every force one object exerts on a second object, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force back on the first. The single most commonly missed detail: these two forces act on two different objects, never on the same one. When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you with equal force — these two forces don’t cancel each other out in any calculation involving just you, or just the wall, because they act on different objects entirely; they only “cancel” in the sense of being equal and opposite as a pair.

The everyday force catalog

  • Weight ((W=mg)): the force of gravity on an object’s mass; always points straight down.
  • Normal force ((N)): the perpendicular push a surface exerts on an object resting against it — a reaction force that adjusts itself to whatever is needed to prevent the object from passing through the surface. Critically, normal force is not always equal to weight — it depends on context.

Worked example (why normal force isn’t always weight): An elevator accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s². Find the normal force on a 60 kg passenger.

Two forces act on the passenger: weight (down) and the normal force from the floor (up). Newton’s second law, taking up as positive:

\[N - mg = ma \Rightarrow N = m(g+a) = 60(9.8+1.5) = 678\text{ N}\]

Compare this to the passenger’s actual weight, (mg=60(9.8)=588) N — the normal force is larger than weight here, which matches the familiar sensation of feeling heavier as an elevator accelerates upward.

  • Tension ((T)): the pulling force transmitted along a rope, string, or cable, always directed away from the object, along the line of the rope.

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming normal force always equals (mg). This shortcut only works for an object on a flat, horizontal, non-accelerating surface with no other vertical forces present. The moment there’s a vertical acceleration (elevator), an additional applied force, or a tilted surface, normal force must be solved for using Newton’s second law directly — never simply assumed equal to weight.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • First law: zero net force means zero acceleration (constant velocity, possibly zero).
  • Second law: \(F_{net}=ma\) — the central tool going forward.
  • Third law: action-reaction pairs are equal and opposite, but act on two different objects.
  • Normal force is a reaction force determined by context — it is not automatically equal to weight.

Class Day 14: F = ma in Multiple Contexts

Why this matters

Real systems often involve more than one object linked together — two blocks connected by a string, for instance. Today extends F = ma from single isolated objects to connected systems, where the key new insight is that connected objects share a common acceleration.

The shared-acceleration trick

If two objects are connected by an inextensible (non-stretching) string over an ideal (massless, frictionless) pulley, they must have the same magnitude of acceleration at every instant — if one speeds up, the string forces the other to speed up by exactly the same amount, since the string itself can’t stretch or go slack. This shared-acceleration condition is what lets you solve two-object systems: write Newton’s second law separately for each object, then combine the two equations using the fact that both accelerations are equal (in magnitude).

The classic setup: two hanging masses (Atwood machine)

Two masses, \(m_1\) and \(m_2\), hang from either end of a string over a pulley, with \(m_2 > m_1\) (so \(m_2\) will accelerate downward and \(m_1\) upward). Draw a separate free-body diagram for each mass:

For \(m_1\) (accelerating upward, taking up as positive for this mass): \(T - m_1g = m_1a\)

For \(m_2\) (accelerating downward, taking down as positive for this mass, so the signs work out consistently with a shared positive (a)): \(m_2g - T = m_2a\)

Adding these two equations together makes the unknown tension (T) cancel out:

\[m_2g - m_1g = m_1a+m_2a \Rightarrow a = \frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2}\]

Once (a) is known, substitute back into either original equation to find (T).

Worked example: Two blocks (4 kg and 9 kg) are connected by a string over a frictionless, massless pulley, with the 9 kg block hanging. Find the acceleration and tension.

\[a = \frac{(9-4)(9.8)}{4+9} = \frac{49}{13} = 3.77\text{ m/s}^2\]

Substituting into the 4 kg block’s equation ((T-0=4a), since it’s on a frictionless table, so only tension acts horizontally on it): (T = 4(3.77) = 15.1) N.

Extending to more than two objects

The same principle scales up: three blocks connected in a line and pulled by one force all share the same acceleration, and you can either treat the whole line as one combined system (total mass, total applied force) to find the shared acceleration quickly, or analyze each block individually (useful when you need to find the tension between two specific blocks, which requires isolating one of them and applying Newton’s second law to it alone).

Common mistake to watch for

Assigning inconsistent positive directions to the two objects in a hanging-mass system. Since the two masses move in physically different directions (one up, one down) but must have numerically equal acceleration magnitudes, you must choose “positive” separately for each object in a way that’s consistent with the direction it will actually accelerate — otherwise the two equations won’t combine correctly, and canceling (T) will give the wrong sign or wrong magnitude for (a).

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Objects connected by an inextensible string over an ideal pulley share the same acceleration magnitude.
  • Atwood machine: (a = ), derived by adding the two objects’ separate Newton’s-second-law equations.
  • For multi-block systems, treat the whole chain as one system for the shared acceleration, or isolate individual blocks to find specific inter-block tensions.

Class Day 15: Inclined Planes

Why this matters

Today combines Chapter 3’s incline decomposition directly with Newton’s second law, producing one of the most common problem setups in all of introductory mechanics: an object sliding on an incline.

Setting up the frictionless case

Using the incline-aligned axes from Chapter 3 Day 2, decompose gravity into \(W_\parallel=mg\sin\theta\) (down the slope) and \(W_\perp=mg\cos\theta\) (into the surface). Perpendicular to the incline, nothing accelerates (the object stays on the surface), so the normal force exactly balances \(W_\perp\): (N=mg).

Along the incline, assuming no friction, the only force is \(W_\parallel\), so Newton’s second law gives:

\[ma = mg\sin\theta \Rightarrow a = g\sin\theta\]

The result worth sitting with: mass cancels

Notice that mass completely cancels out of the final formula for acceleration. This mirrors free fall from Chapter 2 (where all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass) — an incline is, in a sense, a “diluted” version of free fall, with the full acceleration (g) reduced by a factor of \(\sin\theta\) because only part of gravity acts along the sliding direction. A heavier block and a lighter block released from rest on the same frictionless incline will slide down side-by-side, reaching the bottom at exactly the same time.

Worked example: A 15 kg box slides down a frictionless 32° incline. Find its acceleration and the normal force.

\[a = g\sin32° = 9.8(0.530) = 5.19\text{ m/s}^2 \qquad N = mg\cos32° = 15(9.8)(0.848) = 124.7\text{ N}\]

Note the acceleration didn’t need the mass at all — only the normal force calculation did.

Adding an applied force along the incline

If an additional force acts along the incline surface (say, someone pushing the block up the slope, or a rope pulling it), simply add that force into the along-incline Newton’s-second-law equation, with appropriate sign depending on whether it points up-slope or down-slope:

\[ma = F_{applied} - mg\sin\theta \qquad \text{(if the applied force acts up-slope)}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting that the perpendicular direction still needs its own equation (even though nothing accelerates there) — students sometimes skip writing (N=mg) since “nothing happens” perpendicular to the incline, but you’ll need that normal force value later (Day 4, when friction is introduced, since (f=N) depends directly on it).

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Frictionless incline: (a=g), independent of mass.
  • Normal force on an incline (no other vertical forces): (N=mg).
  • Any additional along-incline applied force adds directly into the parallel-direction Newton’s-second-law equation, with sign depending on its direction.

Class Day 16: Frictional Forces

Why this matters

Friction is the force that resists relative sliding between two surfaces in contact, and it’s the piece that turns yesterday’s idealized frictionless inclines into realistic ones. Today introduces the friction model used throughout the rest of the course.

Static vs. kinetic friction

Static friction acts on objects that are not sliding, and adjusts itself (up to a maximum) to prevent sliding from starting at all. Kinetic friction acts on objects that are already sliding, opposing the ongoing relative motion.

\[f_{s,max} = \mu_sN \qquad f_k = \mu_kN\]

where \(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_k\) are the coefficients of static and kinetic friction (dimensionless numbers depending on the two materials in contact), and (N) is the normal force. Generally \(\mu_s \geq \mu_k\) for the same pair of surfaces, which matches everyday experience: it typically takes more force to start something sliding than to keep it sliding once it’s already moving.

Adding kinetic friction to the incline

Returning to yesterday’s frictionless incline result and adding kinetic friction (opposing the down-slope sliding motion, so acting up-slope):

\[ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_kmg\cos\theta \Rightarrow a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)\]

Worked example: An 18 kg crate on a 26° incline experiences kinetic friction with \(\mu_k=0.22\). Find its acceleration.

\[a = 9.8(\sin26° - 0.22\cos26°) = 9.8(0.438-0.22\times0.899) = 9.8(0.438-0.198) = 2.36\text{ m/s}^2\]

The static threshold: will it even start sliding?

For an object resting on an incline, it remains stationary as long as the maximum available static friction is enough to balance the down-slope pull of gravity: \(\mu_sN \geq mg\sin\theta\). Since (N=mg), this simplifies beautifully — mass cancels again:

\[\mu_s \geq \tan\theta\]

This gives a clean physical meaning to the coefficient of static friction: it’s directly related to the steepest angle a given pair of surfaces can support without sliding. If you know \(\mu_s\) for a surface, (^{-1}\(\mu_s\)) tells you the incline angle at which an object on that surface is right on the verge of sliding.

Worked example: Determine the minimum coefficient of static friction needed to keep a 10 kg box stationary on a 24° incline.

\[\mu_{s,min} = \tan24° = 0.445\]

Common mistake to watch for

Using \(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_k\) interchangeably. They describe genuinely different physical situations (about-to-slide vs. already-sliding), are usually different numerical values for the same surfaces, and mixing them up (especially using \(\mu_s\) inside a kinetic-friction force calculation, or vice versa) is a very common source of a wrong numeric answer even when the setup and reasoning are otherwise correct.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • \(f_{s,max}=\mu_sN\) (prevents sliding, up to this maximum); \(f_k=\mu_kN\) (opposes ongoing sliding). Generally \(\mu_s\geq\mu_k\).
  • Incline with kinetic friction: (a=g\(\sin\theta-\mu_k\cos\theta\)).
  • Threshold for remaining static on an incline: \(\mu_s\) (mass cancels).

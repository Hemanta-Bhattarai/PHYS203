Chapter 10: Waves

Class Day 30: Wave Properties

Why this matters

A wave is a way of transporting energy from one place to another without transporting matter along with it — ripples on a pond move outward, but a floating leaf mostly just bobs up and down in place, rather than being carried along. Today establishes the core vocabulary and the single equation that ties wave properties together.

Core vocabulary

  • Wavelength (\(\lambda\)): the spatial distance between two consecutive identical points on the wave (crest to crest, for instance).
  • Frequency ((f)): how many wave cycles pass a fixed point per second.
  • Amplitude: the maximum displacement from equilibrium at any point on the wave — this determines how much energy the wave carries, but (importantly) not its speed.
  • Wave speed ((v)): how fast the wave pattern itself propagates through the medium.

The wave equation

\[v = f\lambda\]

This single equation connects all the core wave properties. A crucial and often-missed point: wave speed is generally set by properties of the medium (its stiffness, density, tension, etc. — not by the source that created the wave). If a source vibrates faster (increasing (f)) while the wave travels through the same medium (so (v) stays fixed), the wave equation tells us wavelength must correspondingly decrease, since (v=f) must still hold with (v) unchanged.

Worked example: A wave has frequency 12 Hz and wavelength 2.5 m. Find its speed.

\[v = f\lambda = 12(2.5) = 30\text{ m/s}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming a louder or larger-amplitude wave necessarily travels faster. Amplitude relates to the energy a wave carries, not its speed — wave speed is a property of the medium the wave is passing through (and, for some media, the wave’s frequency, in cases of dispersion), essentially independent of the wave’s amplitude for the situations covered in this course.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Wavelength (spatial repeat distance), frequency (cycles per second), amplitude (max displacement, related to energy carried), wave speed (how fast the pattern moves).
  • Wave equation: (v=f).
  • Wave speed is generally determined by the medium, not by the source’s frequency; changing frequency (same medium) changes wavelength instead, keeping (v) fixed.

Class Day 31: Superposition, Interference, and Wave Types

Why this matters

When two or more waves occupy the same space at the same time, something remarkably simple happens: their effects just add together. This single idea, the principle of superposition, explains everything from noise-canceling headphones to the complex tone of a musical chord.

The principle of superposition

When waves overlap, the total displacement at any point and any instant is simply the sum of the individual displacements each wave would have produced on its own, as if the other waves weren’t there at all. Two special cases are worth naming:

  • Constructive interference: waves arrive in phase (crests aligning with crests), and their amplitudes add, producing a larger combined wave.
  • Destructive interference: waves arrive out of phase (crest aligning with trough), and their amplitudes subtract, potentially canceling out almost entirely if the amplitudes are equal.

Worked example: Two waves of amplitude 6 cm and 4 cm interfere constructively. Find the resulting amplitude.

Constructive interference simply adds the amplitudes directly (when perfectly in phase): (6+4=10) cm.

Real-world application: noise-canceling headphones

This technology is a direct, practical application of destructive interference: a microphone detects incoming ambient noise, and the headphones generate a new sound wave that is the same amplitude but precisely out of phase with that noise. When the two waves superpose at your ear, they very nearly cancel out, leaving near-silence where the ambient noise used to be — this is engineering built entirely on the superposition principle.

Transverse vs. longitudinal waves

This is a classification based on the relationship between the oscillation direction and the direction the wave travels:

  • Transverse waves: the medium oscillates perpendicular to the direction of wave travel (waves on a string; light).
  • Longitudinal waves: the medium oscillates parallel to (along) the direction of wave travel (sound; a Slinky pushed and pulled along its length).

A Slinky is a great physical demonstration of both: shake one end side-to-side, and a transverse wave travels down it; instead push-and-pull one end along its length, and a longitudinal (compression) wave travels down it instead.

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming destructive interference “destroys” the energy carried by the interfering waves. It doesn’t — energy is still conserved overall; destructive interference at one specific location is typically accompanied by extra constructive interference somewhere else nearby (energy is redistributed in space, not annihilated).

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Superposition: overlapping waves simply add their displacements at every point.
  • Constructive interference (in phase) increases amplitude; destructive interference (out of phase) decreases or cancels it.
  • Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to travel direction (string, light); longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to travel direction (sound).

Class Day 32: Traveling and Standing Waves

Why this matters

Most waves you can directly picture (ripples spreading outward, a wave on a rope you shake) are traveling waves — they carry a moving pattern from one place to another. But when a wave reflects and overlaps with itself, something quite different can emerge: a standing wave, which appears to oscillate in place rather than travel anywhere.

How standing waves arise

A standing wave is the direct result of superposition (Day 2) applied to two identical waves traveling in opposite directions — commonly, a wave traveling one direction and its own reflection traveling back the other way, along the same medium (like a string fixed at both ends). Where these two oppositely-traveling waves consistently cancel, you get a fixed point of zero motion, called a node; where they consistently reinforce, you get a point of maximum oscillation, called an antinode. Unlike a traveling wave, the overall pattern of nodes and antinodes stays fixed in place, even as the medium at each antinode continues oscillating up and down.

Normal modes on a fixed string

A string fixed at both ends (like a guitar string) must have a node at each fixed end (since those points genuinely cannot move). This constraint means only specific, discrete wavelengths can “fit” on the string — not just any wavelength will do. The lowest-frequency mode that fits, called the fundamental (or first harmonic), has the longest possible wavelength consistent with nodes at both ends: exactly twice the string’s length, \(\lambda_1=2L\). Higher modes (second harmonic, third harmonic, etc.) fit progressively shorter wavelengths, each an integer fraction of that fundamental wavelength.

Worked example: A guitar string of length 0.65 m is tuned so its fundamental frequency is 330 Hz. Find the wave speed on the string.

Fundamental wavelength: (_1=2L=2(0.65)=1.3) m. Using the wave equation from Day 1:

\[v = f\lambda = 330(1.3) = 429\text{ m/s}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Thinking a standing wave transports no energy at all, anywhere. In the idealized (lossless) case, energy doesn’t travel net from one end of the standing wave pattern to the other, but energy is still actively oscillating back and forth locally between kinetic and potential forms at each antinode — “standing” describes the fixed spatial pattern, not a complete absence of motion or energy within the system.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • A standing wave arises from superposition of two identical waves traveling in opposite directions (commonly, a wave and its own reflection).
  • Nodes (fixed, zero-motion points) and antinodes (maximum-oscillation points) define the standing wave pattern, which does not itself travel.
  • A string fixed at both ends supports discrete normal modes; the fundamental has wavelength \(\lambda_1=2L\), the longest wavelength consistent with nodes at both ends.

Chapter 4: Two-Dimensional Motion

Class Day 10: Simple 2D Motion

Why this matters

This is the day where Chapters 2 and 3 finally merge. Everything you learned about position, velocity, and acceleration in 1D still applies — you’re just going to apply it twice, once for the x-direction and once for the y-direction, entirely independently of each other.

The key idea: independence

If the acceleration in the x-direction, \(a_x\), doesn’t depend on what’s happening in the y-direction (and vice versa), then the x-motion and y-motion can be solved as two completely separate 1D problems, each using the exact same tools from Chapter 2, and then recombined at the end using vector addition.

Position becomes a vector: (r(t) = (x(t), y(t))). Velocity and acceleration are found by differentiating each component separately:

\[\vec v(t) = \left(\frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt}\right) \qquad \vec a(t) = \left(\frac{d^2x}{dt^2},\ \frac{d^2y}{dt^2}\right)\]

Worked example: An object has position (x(t)=4t), (y(t)=5t-2t^2). Find its velocity components at (t=1)s.

\[v_x(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(4t) = 4 \qquad v_y(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(5t-2t^2) = 5-4t\]

At (t=1): \(v_x=4\) m/s (constant, unchanged), (v_y = 5-4(1)=1) m/s.

Recombining into a single speed or direction

Once you have both velocity components at some instant, the object’s actual speed (a scalar) and direction come from the same magnitude/angle formulas you learned in Chapter 3:

\[\text{speed} = \sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2} \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right)\]

A common and important error to avoid: you cannot find the speed by simply adding \(v_x\) and \(v_y\) directly. Speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector, which requires the Pythagorean combination, not simple addition — adding the components directly conflates a vector quantity with its magnitude and will give a wrong (usually too large) answer.

What kind of path results?

If both \(a_x\) and \(a_y\) are zero (no acceleration at all), the object moves in a straight line at constant velocity. If one direction has acceleration and the other doesn’t (as in the worked example above, where \(a_x=0\) but \(a_y=-4\)), the resulting path is generally a curve — specifically, a parabola, since one coordinate grows linearly in time while the other involves a \(t^2\) term. This exact situation — constant velocity horizontally, constant acceleration vertically — is precisely what tomorrow’s topic, projectile motion, is built on.

Common mistake to watch for

Trying to solve the x and y motion “together” as one combined equation, rather than treating them as two separate, parallel 1D problems that happen to share the same clock ((t)). The whole benefit of the independence principle is that you don’t need any new mathematical tools for 2D motion — you just apply Chapter 2’s toolkit twice and combine the results at the end with vector addition.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • (r(t)=(x(t),y(t))); differentiate each component separately for velocity and acceleration.
  • If \(a_x\) and \(a_y\) don’t depend on each other, x-motion and y-motion can be solved independently using 1D kinematics tools.
  • Speed = \(\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\) — never simply \(v_x+v_y\).

Class Day 11: Projectile Motion

Why this matters

Projectile motion is the single most direct application of yesterday’s independence principle, and one of the most common problem types in all of introductory physics: an object launched into the air, moving under gravity alone (no air resistance), tracing out the classic arc shape.

Setting up the problem

The key move is decomposing the initial launch velocity into horizontal and vertical components right at the start:

\[v_{x0} = v_0\cos\theta \qquad v_{y0} = v_0\sin\theta\]

From there, independence takes over completely:

  • Horizontal motion: constant velocity (no horizontal acceleration, assuming no air resistance): (x(t) = v_{x0}t).
  • Vertical motion: free fall, exactly as in Chapter 2 Day 4, with \(a_y=-g\): (y(t) = v_{y0}t - 12gt^2).

Everything else in this topic — time of flight, maximum height, range — is just applying Chapter 2’s kinematic toolkit to these two equations.

The three headline results

Time of flight (for launch and landing at the same height): set (y=0) and solve for the nonzero root:

\[0 = v_{y0}t-\tfrac12gt^2 \Rightarrow t_{flight} = \frac{2v_{y0}}{g}\]

Maximum height (where \(v_y=0\), the vertical analog of the ball-thrown-up peak from Chapter 2):

\[h_{max} = \frac{v_{y0}^2}{2g}\]

Range (horizontal distance at landing): combine the time of flight with the constant horizontal velocity:

\[R = v_{x0}\times t_{flight} = v_{x0}\times\frac{2v_{y0}}{g} = \frac{2v_0^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}\]

(the last step uses the trig identity (2=(2)), which is where the compact range formula comes from).

Worked example: A projectile is launched at 24 m/s at 40° from level ground. Find its range, maximum height, and time of flight.

\[v_{x0}=24\cos40°=18.4\text{ m/s}, \qquad v_{y0}=24\sin40°=15.4\text{ m/s}\] \[t_{flight}=\frac{2(15.4)}{9.8}=3.15\text{ s}, \qquad h_{max}=\frac{15.4^2}{2(9.8)}=12.1\text{ m}, \qquad R=18.4\times3.15=58.0\text{ m}\]

The complementary-angle symmetry

Because ((2)) is symmetric about 45° (i.e., ((2°)=(60°)=(120°)=(2°))), any two launch angles that add up to 90° (like 30°/60°, or 20°/70°) produce the exact same range for the same launch speed, on level ground. The maximum range for a fixed launch speed occurs at exactly 45°, since that’s where ((2)) reaches its peak value of 1.

This symmetry breaks, however, the moment launch and landing heights differ (e.g., launching from an elevated platform down to ground level) — in that case, the optimal angle for maximum range shifts to something less than 45°, since the projectile spends extra unpaired time falling the additional height with only its (unchanging) horizontal velocity acting.

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting that the simple \(t_{flight}=2v_{y0}/g\) and range formulas above only apply when launch and landing heights are equal. The moment a problem describes a launch from a cliff, platform, or building, you must go back to the general vertical position equation, \(y=v_{y0}t-\tfrac12gt^2\), set (y) equal to whatever the actual height difference is (often negative, if landing below the launch point), and solve the resulting quadratic directly — the shortcut formulas no longer apply.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • \(v_{x0}=v_0\), \(v_{y0}=v_0\); horizontal motion is constant-velocity, vertical motion is free fall.
  • Time of flight \(t=2v_{y0}/g\), max height (=v_{y0}^2/(2g)), range (=v_0^2(2)/g) — valid only for equal launch/landing height.
  • Complementary launch angles (summing to 90°) give equal range on level ground; 45° maximizes range in that case.

Class Day 12: Uniform Circular Motion

Why this matters

Circular motion introduces a genuinely new and often counterintuitive idea: an object can be accelerating even while moving at a perfectly constant speed. Reconciling this with the everyday sense of “acceleration means speeding up or slowing down” is the main conceptual hurdle of the day.

The apparent paradox, resolved

Acceleration is defined as the rate of change of velocity — and velocity is a vector, carrying both magnitude (speed) and direction. In uniform circular motion, the speed genuinely never changes, but the direction of the velocity vector is constantly changing, sweeping around the circle. Since velocity is changing (even though speed alone isn’t), there must be a nonzero acceleration — it’s just entirely a change in direction, not magnitude.

Centripetal acceleration

This direction-only acceleration is called centripetal acceleration (“center-seeking”), and it always points from the object directly toward the center of the circle, perpendicular to the velocity at every instant. Its magnitude is:

\[a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}\]

where (v) is the (constant) speed and (r) is the radius of the circular path.

Worked example: A 1500 kg car rounds a curve of radius 60 m at 18 m/s. Find the centripetal acceleration and the force required.

\[a_c = \frac{18^2}{60} = 5.4\text{ m/s}^2\]

Using Newton’s second law (previewing Chapter 5): (F_c = ma_c = 1500(5.4) = 8100).

Centripetal force is a role, not a new kind of force

This is the single most important conceptual point of the day: “centripetal force” is not a new, separate type of force alongside gravity, tension, friction, and normal force — it’s simply the name given to whichever real force (or combination of forces) happens to be supplying the center-pointing acceleration in a given situation. For a car rounding a curve, friction between the tires and road provides the centripetal force. For a ball on a string swung in a horizontal circle, the string’s tension provides it. For a satellite orbiting Earth, gravity itself provides it. The formula \(F_c=mv^2/r\) tells you how much center-pointing force is needed; identifying which real force is doing that job is a separate, physical question you must answer by looking at the actual setup.

Common mistake to watch for

Drawing a free-body diagram with an additional outward “centrifugal force” arrow, on top of the real forces present. In the (non-rotating, inertial) reference frame used throughout this course, there is no such outward force — the sensation of being “pushed outward” in a turning car is really just your body’s inertia (tending to continue in a straight line) combined with the car seat/door pushing you inward, toward the center, to keep you on the curved path along with the car.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • In uniform circular motion, speed is constant but velocity direction continuously changes, producing a nonzero acceleration.
  • Centripetal acceleration: \(a_c=v^2/r\), always directed toward the center.
  • Centripetal force, \(F_c=mv^2/r\), is the net inward force required — it is supplied by whatever real force (tension, friction, gravity, etc.) is present in a given problem, not a separate force in its own right.

Chapter 3: Vectors

Class Day 8: Vector Representation, Components, Addition, Subtraction, Scalar Multiplication

Why this matters

Every quantity you’ve dealt with so far in this course (position, velocity, acceleration in 1D) has secretly been a vector, but in one dimension a vector only needs a single sign (+ or −) to capture its direction. Once we move to two and three dimensions — which is most of the physical world — we need a proper mathematical object that carries both magnitude and direction simultaneously. That object is a vector, and today is about learning to manipulate them.

Scalars vs. vectors

A scalar is a quantity fully described by a single number and a unit: mass, temperature, time, distance, speed. A vector additionally requires a direction to be fully specified: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force. Saying “the car moved 5 km” is incomplete; saying “the car moved 5 km northeast” is a vector statement.

Two equivalent representations

A vector can be described in two completely equivalent ways, and fluently converting between them is the core skill of this unit.

Magnitude-angle form: a length (A) and an angle \(\theta\) (measured from some reference direction, usually the positive x-axis).

Component form: an x-component and a y-component, (\(A_x, A_y\)), describing how far the vector reaches along each axis.

Converting from magnitude-angle to components uses basic trigonometry:

\[A_x = A\cos\theta \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta\]

Converting the other way, from components back to magnitude-angle, uses the Pythagorean theorem and arctangent:

\[A = \sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2} \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)\]

with a caveat: the plain arctangent function only returns angles in a limited range, so you must check which quadrant (\(A_x,A_y\)) actually lies in and adjust the angle accordingly if the calculator’s raw output doesn’t match.

Worked example: A vector has components ((-6, 8)). Find its magnitude and direction.

\[A = \sqrt{(-6)^2+8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10\]

For direction, (^{-1}(8/-6)) — but since \(A_x<0\) and \(A_y>0\), this vector points into the second quadrant (up and to the left), so the raw calculator angle needs 180° added to land in the correct quadrant, giving (°).

Adding and subtracting vectors

Graphically, vectors add head-to-tail: draw the first vector, then start the second vector’s tail exactly where the first vector’s head ended. The resultant is the single arrow drawn from the very first tail to the very last head.

Algebraically (and this is almost always faster and less error-prone), adding vectors just means adding their respective components separately:

\[\vec A + \vec B = (A_x+B_x,\ A_y+B_y)\]

Subtraction is addition of the negative: (A - B = A + (-B)), where (-B) simply means flipping the sign of every component of (B) (equivalent to rotating it 180°).

Worked example: Add (A = (5,-3)) and (B=(-2,7)), and find the resultant’s magnitude and direction.

\[\vec A + \vec B = (5+(-2),\ -3+7) = (3,4)\] \[|R| = \sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5, \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}(4/3) = 53.1°\]

Scalar multiplication

Multiplying a vector by a scalar (k) scales every component by that same number: (kA = \(kA_x, kA_y\)). If (k) is positive, the direction is unchanged and the magnitude simply scales by (k). If (k) is negative, the direction reverses by exactly 180° in addition to scaling the magnitude by (|k|).

Common mistake to watch for

A frequent slip is computing the magnitude of a sum by adding the two original magnitudes directly (i.e., assuming (|A+B| = |A|+|B|)). This is only true in the special case where both vectors point in exactly the same direction; in general, you must add components first, then take the magnitude of the result — never add magnitudes directly unless the vectors are parallel.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • \(A_x=A\), \(A_y=A\); and inversely, (A=), (=^{-1}\(A_y/A_x\)) (checking quadrant).
  • Vector addition/subtraction: add or subtract components separately, never add magnitudes directly (except when parallel).
  • Scalar multiplication scales every component by the same factor; a negative scalar also reverses direction by 180°.

Class Day 9: Vector Decomposition

Why this matters

Yesterday we decomposed vectors along the standard horizontal/vertical axes. Today’s key insight is that nothing forces you to use horizontal and vertical — you’re free to choose whatever pair of perpendicular axes makes a given problem simplest, and for problems involving inclined surfaces, tilting your axes to match the incline dramatically simplifies everything that follows (particularly once we reach Newton’s laws in Chapter 5).

Why tilt the axes at all?

Picture a block resting on a ramp. If you insist on using standard horizontal/vertical axes, the normal force (which always points perpendicular to the surface) has both a horizontal and a vertical component — messy. But if you instead choose one axis running along the incline surface and the other perpendicular to it, the normal force becomes purely one-dimensional (entirely along your new perpendicular axis), and gravity becomes the vector that needs decomposing instead. This trade is almost always a good one, since gravity’s direction is fixed and simple (straight down), making it easy to decompose onto tilted axes, while the normal force’s direction depends on the incline and is awkward to decompose onto standard axes.

Decomposing weight on an incline

For an incline at angle \(\theta\) (measured from the horizontal), the weight vector (mg), which always points straight down, splits into:

\[W_{\parallel} = mg\sin\theta \qquad \text{(component along the incline surface, driving sliding)}\] \[W_{\perp} = mg\cos\theta \qquad \text{(component pressing into the incline surface, balanced by the normal force)}\]

It’s worth actually deriving this geometrically rather than just memorizing it, since the sin/cos assignment is easy to swap by mistake. Draw the incline as a right triangle with angle \(\theta\) at the base. The weight vector points straight down from the block. The angle between the weight vector and the perpendicular-to-incline direction turns out to equal \(\theta\) itself (this follows from the fact that the incline surface and the ground are related by a rotation of \(\theta\), and the weight vector’s true direction — straight down — doesn’t rotate with it). From there, basic right-triangle trigonometry gives the perpendicular component as (mg) (adjacent side) and the parallel component as (mg) (opposite side).

Worked example: A 25 kg crate rests on a 35° incline. Find both weight components.

\[W_{\parallel} = 25(9.8)\sin35° = 140.5\text{ N} \qquad W_{\perp} = 25(9.8)\cos35° = 200.7\text{ N}\]

Sanity checks using limiting cases

A powerful way to verify you have sin and cos assigned to the right component is to check the extremes. At (°) (a flat, horizontal surface), there should be no tendency to slide, so \(W_\parallel\) should be zero: indeed, (mg°=0). ✓. At (°) (an idealized vertical wall), gravity should act entirely along the surface (straight down, parallel to the wall) with nothing pressing into it, so \(W_\perp\) should be zero: indeed, (mg°=0). ✓. If your formulas don’t pass these two sanity checks, you have sin and cos swapped.

Generalizing beyond weight

The same tilted-axis approach works for any vector, not just weight — an applied force, a tension, a horizontal push — all can be decomposed onto incline-aligned axes the same way, just being careful about the actual angle between that specific vector and your chosen axes (which may not simply be \(\theta\) or (90°-) if the vector isn’t purely vertical or purely along the incline to begin with).

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting that the normal force is perpendicular to the actual surface, not simply “straight up” — on a tilted incline these are different directions, and drawing the normal force vertically is a very common diagram error that then propagates into every subsequent equation.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • On an incline at angle \(\theta\): \(W_\parallel=mg\sin\theta\) (down-slope), \(W_\perp= mg\cos\theta\) (into the surface, balanced by the normal force).
  • Sanity check using (°) and (°) limiting cases to confirm sin/cos are assigned correctly.
  • Any vector, not just weight, can be decomposed onto conveniently chosen tilted axes.

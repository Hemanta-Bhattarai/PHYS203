Chapter 1: Units and Dimensions

Class Day 2: Units

Why this matters

Every measurement in physics is a comparison against some agreed-upon standard. When you say a table is “1.5 meters long,” you’re really saying “this table is 1.5 times as long as a specific reference length called a meter.” Without that shared reference, the number 1.5 means nothing at all. This is the entire point of a system of units: it lets a measurement made in one place, by one person, mean the same thing to everyone else, everywhere.

SI base units

The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units. For this course, three matter constantly:

Quantity SI base unit Symbol
Length meter m
Mass kilogram kg
Time second s

(The other four — ampere, kelvin, mole, candela — cover electric current, temperature, amount of substance, and luminous intensity, and will matter more in later courses.)

Everything else you’ll compute in this course — velocity, force, energy, momentum — is a derived unit, built by combining these three (and occasionally the others) through multiplication and division. For example: - Velocity: meters per second, m/s - Force (the newton): \(1\text{ N} = 1\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\) - Energy (the joule): \(1\text{ J} = 1\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{/s}^2\)

A newton and a joule look like fundamental things because they have their own names, but underneath, they’re just combinations of kilograms, meters, and seconds. Keep this in mind — it will matter a great deal in Chapter 1, Day 2 (Dimensions), and it’s the reason F = ma “generates” the newton as a unit rather than the newton being something separate that needs its own conversion rule.

The technique: unit conversion by multiplying by 1

The single most useful trick in this entire day is this: any true equality can be turned into a fraction equal to 1. Since (1 = 100), it’s also true that:

\[\frac{100\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}} = 1 \qquad \text{and equally} \qquad \frac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ cm}} = 1\]

Multiplying any quantity by either of these fractions doesn’t change its actual value — it only changes which units it’s expressed in, because you’re multiplying by 1. This is the whole secret behind unit conversion: you choose the version of the fraction (numerator vs. denominator) that makes the unit you want to get rid of cancel out, and leaves you with the unit you want.

Worked example: Convert 5 km to meters.

We know (1 = 1000), so ( = 1). Multiply:

\[5\text{ km} \times \frac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}} = 5000\text{ m}\]

Notice the km in the original quantity cancels with the km in the denominator of the fraction, leaving only meters. That cancellation is not a coincidence — it’s the entire reason the method works, and it’s worth narrating out loud (“km cancels with km, leaving m”) every time you do this until it becomes automatic.

Worked example (multi-step): Convert 60 mph to m/s.

Here we need to change two units at once: miles → meters, and hours → seconds. The trick still works — we just chain two conversion factors together:

\[60\ \frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1609\text{ m}}{1\text{ mi}} \times \frac{1\text{ h}}{3600\text{ s}} = 26.8\ \text{m/s}\]

Walk through the cancellation: “mi” in the original numerator cancels with “mi” in the second fraction’s denominator; “h” in the original denominator cancels with “h” in the third fraction’s numerator. What’s left is m in the numerator and s in the denominator — exactly m/s, the unit we wanted. The order you multiply the fractions in doesn’t matter; what matters is that each fraction is built so that one of its units matches (and cancels) something already present.

Common mistake to watch for

The most common error is writing the conversion factor upside down — for instance, multiplying by \(\dfrac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}}\) when you actually needed \(\dfrac{1\text{ km}}{1000\text{ m}}\). The fix isn’t to memorize which way is “correct” — it’s to always check that the unit you want to eliminate is the one that appears in the position (numerator or denominator) that lets it cancel. If your units don’t cancel to leave what you want, the fraction is upside down; flip it.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • (1 = 1000); (1 = 100); (1 = 3600); (1 )
  • Any true equality can be written as a fraction equal to 1, and multiplying by it never changes a quantity’s actual value — only its units.

Class Day 3: Dimensions

Why this matters

Where Day 1 was about converting between specific units (feet vs. meters, hours vs. seconds), today is about something one level more abstract: the type of quantity itself, independent of which unit system you’re using. This distinction — dimension vs. unit — is subtle the first time you see it, so let’s be precise.

Dimension vs. unit

A unit is a specific, chosen scale for measuring something (meters, feet, miles are all units of the same dimension: length). A dimension is the fundamental category a quantity belongs to, written using bracket notation:

  • Length: ([L])
  • Mass: ([M])
  • Time: ([T])

Velocity, no matter whether you measure it in m/s, mph, or furlongs per fortnight, always has the dimension \([L][T]^{-1}\) — “length divided by time.” The unit changes depending on what scale you pick; the dimension never does, because it describes the underlying physical nature of the quantity, not the number attached to it.

You can find the dimension of any quantity by looking at its defining equation and substituting dimensions for each symbol. For example, since (v = x/t):

\[[v] = \frac{[L]}{[T]} = [L][T]^{-1}\]

And since acceleration is (a = v/t):

\[[a] = \frac{[L][T]^{-1}}{[T]} = [L][T]^{-2}\]

And since force is (F=ma):

\[[F] = [M]\times[L][T]^{-2} = [M][L][T]^{-2}\]

Dimensional analysis as a sanity check

The central use of dimensions in this course is as a fast, free error-check on any equation: every term that is added, subtracted, or set equal to another term must share the exact same dimension. You cannot add a length to a time any more than you can add three apples to five oranges and call the answer “eight” of something meaningful.

Worked example: Check whether \(x = v_0t + \tfrac12at^2\) is dimensionally consistent.

Break it into three terms and find the dimension of each: - (x): dimension ([L]) - \(v_0t\): \([L][T]^{-1}= [L]\) - \(\tfrac12at^2\): the \(\tfrac12\) is a pure number with no dimension, so this reduces to \([L][T]^{-2}\times[T]^2 = [L]\)

All three terms reduce to ([L]). Since everything matches, the equation passes the dimensional check — it could be correct (and it is; this is one of the standard kinematic equations).

Worked counterexample: Check whether \(x = v_0t^2\) could be a valid equation for position.

\[[v_0t^2] = [L][T]^{-1}\times[T]^2 = [L][T]\]

This is ([L][T]), not ([L]). Since it doesn’t match the dimension of (x), this equation is dimensionally inconsistent and therefore cannot be correct as written, regardless of what numerical coefficient you might try attaching to it.

The crucial limitation: what dimensional analysis can’t catch

This is the single most commonly misunderstood point on this topic, so it’s worth stating plainly: dimensional analysis cannot detect an error in a pure (dimensionless) numerical factor. If a textbook accidentally printed \(x = v_0t + at^2\) (missing the \(\tfrac12\)), dimensional analysis would say this equation is perfectly fine — because \(\tfrac12\) has no dimension at all, removing or changing it doesn’t affect the dimensional bookkeeping in the slightest. Dimensional analysis is a necessary check (an equation that fails it is definitely wrong) but not a sufficient one (an equation that passes it isn’t guaranteed to be right). Think of it as a spelling-checker for physics equations: it catches certain kinds of errors reliably, but it has no idea whether the sentence actually makes sense.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Dimension of velocity: \([L][T]^{-1}\). Acceleration: \([L][T]^{-2}\). Force: \([M][L][T]^{-2}\). Energy: \([M][L]^2[T]^{-2}\). Momentum: \([M][L][T]^{-1}\).
  • Every additive term in a valid equation must share the same dimension.
  • Dimensional consistency is necessary but not sufficient for an equation to be correct — it cannot catch errors in dimensionless numerical constants.

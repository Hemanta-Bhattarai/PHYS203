Chapter 2: One-Dimensional Kinematics

Class Day 4: Position, Velocity, and Acceleration (Average); Graphical Analysis

Why this matters

Kinematics is the language we use to describe motion precisely, before we ever ask why something moves the way it does (that’s Chapter 5’s job). Everything in this chapter builds toward one goal: being able to look at a graph of an object’s motion and read off exactly what’s happening at any moment, or conversely, being able to sketch a graph from a verbal description. Today’s job is to nail down the vocabulary and connect it to graphs.

Distance vs. displacement — the first fork in the road

Distance is how much ground was covered, total, regardless of direction — it only ever adds up, never subtracts. Displacement is the straight-line change in position, from start to end, and it’s a vector: it can be positive, negative, or zero.

If you walk 5 meters east and then 5 meters back west, your distance traveled is 10 meters, but your displacement is exactly zero — you’re back where you started. This isn’t a technicality; it’s the reason two different, equally valid quantities exist: average speed (total distance / total time) and average velocity (displacement / total time) can give wildly different answers for the same trip.

\[\bar v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_f - x_i}{t_f-t_i} \qquad\qquad \bar{\text{speed}} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}\]

Worked example: A hiker walks 4 km east in 50 minutes, then 4 km west in 70 minutes. Find the average velocity and average speed for the round trip.

Net displacement is (4 - 4 = 0) km, so (v = 0) — no matter how long the trip took, if you end up back at the start, your average velocity is zero. But average speed uses total distance: (4+4=8) km over (50+70=120) min = 2 h, giving ({} = 8/2 = 4) km/h. Same trip, two completely different (and equally correct) numbers, because they’re answering different questions.

Average acceleration

The same logic extends one level up: average acceleration is the change in velocity over the change in time,

\[\bar a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_i}{t_f - t_i}\]

A subtlety worth sitting with: average acceleration only depends on the starting and ending velocities, not on anything that happens in between. An elevator that goes from rest, up to speed, cruises, then decelerates back to rest over its whole trip has an average acceleration of exactly zero over the full ride — even though it clearly accelerated and decelerated along the way. Average acceleration smooths all of that out; it only sees the endpoints.

The graphical connection: secant lines

This is the idea that the entire rest of the chapter builds on, so it’s worth being very deliberate about it. On a position-time graph, pick any two points. Draw the straight line connecting them (this is called a secant line). The slope of that secant line is:

\[\text{slope} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\]

— which is exactly the definition of average velocity. This isn’t an analogy or a coincidence; average velocity is the slope of the secant line connecting the start and end points on a position-time graph, by definition. The same logic applies one level up: the slope of a secant line on a velocity-time graph is average acceleration.

This graphical picture is the seed that Day 2 will grow into instantaneous velocity (by shrinking the secant line down until it becomes a tangent line) — so it’s worth getting comfortable with secant slopes now, since tomorrow’s entire lesson depends on this picture already making sense.

Common mistake to watch for

Students often report a negative “distance” or a negative “speed” when an object moves backward — but distance and speed are never negative; they’re magnitudes. It’s displacement and velocity that carry the sign, because they’re vectors indicating direction. If your answer to a distance or speed question comes out negative, that’s a signal something went wrong upstream (likely you calculated displacement/velocity and mislabeled it).

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Average velocity: (v = x/t) (can be negative). Average speed: total distance/total time (never negative).
  • Average acceleration: (a = v/t) — depends only on initial and final velocity, not the path taken in between.
  • Average velocity = slope of the secant line on a position-time graph; average acceleration = slope of the secant line on a velocity-time graph.

Class Day 5: Instantaneous Velocity/Acceleration; Connecting Graphs to Calculus

Why this matters

Yesterday’s average velocity answers “how fast, overall, across this whole interval?” But often we want to know the answer to a sharper question: how fast is the object moving right now, at this exact instant? That’s instantaneous velocity, and getting there requires one of the most important ideas in all of calculus: the limit.

From secant to tangent

Take the secant line from yesterday, connecting two points on a position-time curve. Now imagine sliding the second point closer and closer to the first, shrinking the time interval (t) smaller and smaller. As (t ), the secant line rotates and settles into a single, specific line that just barely grazes the curve at that one point without crossing it — the tangent line. Its slope is the instantaneous velocity at that exact instant:

\[v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt}\]

That’s it — that’s a derivative. If you’ve taken (or are taking) calculus, this is exactly the limit definition of the derivative, just wearing a physics costume. If calculus is still new to you, the tangent-line picture is the important part to hold onto: instantaneous velocity is “the slope of the curve at a single point,” found by zooming in until the curve looks straight.

The same logic, one level up, defines instantaneous acceleration as the derivative of velocity: (a(t) = dv/dt).

Working with derivatives directly

For polynomial position functions (which cover the vast majority of what you’ll see in this course), there’s a simple mechanical rule: if (x(t) = ct^n), then (v(t) = dx/dt = nct^{n-1}). Apply the same rule again to get acceleration from velocity.

Worked example: Given (x(t) = 2t^2+3t), find (v(t)) and (a(t)), and evaluate both at (t=2)s.

\[v(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(2t^2+3t) = 4t+3 \qquad a(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(4t+3) = 4\]

At (t=2): (v(2)=4(2)+3=11) m/s, and (a(2)=4) m/s² (constant, since it doesn’t depend on (t) at all).

Reading graphs as a connected set

Once you can find derivatives, you can read three related graphs — position, velocity, acceleration — as a single connected story, where each is the slope of the one above it:

  • Where the position graph is flat (zero slope), velocity is zero.
  • Where the position graph is steepest, velocity is at its largest magnitude.
  • Where the velocity graph crosses zero, the object is momentarily at rest (this doesn’t mean acceleration is zero there — often it’s the opposite, as with a ball at the peak of its flight).
  • Where the velocity graph is flat, acceleration is zero.

There’s also a reverse relationship worth knowing now (it’ll matter more later): the area under a velocity-time graph over some interval equals the displacement over that interval. This previews integration, the reverse operation of differentiation, without needing the formal machinery yet.

Common mistake to watch for

A very common error is assuming a curved position-time graph always means the object is accelerating everywhere along it. That’s not quite right — what matters is whether the slope of the velocity graph (not the position graph) is changing. In fact, a cubic like (x(t)=t^3) has an acceleration that is exactly zero at (t=0), even though the curve is clearly bending on both sides of that point. Curvature in position doesn’t automatically mean nonzero acceleration at every single point within it.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Instantaneous velocity: (v(t) = dx/dt), the slope of the tangent line to the position graph.
  • Instantaneous acceleration: (a(t) = dv/dt), the slope of the tangent line to the velocity graph.
  • Power rule: if (x(t)=ct^n), then \(dx/dt = nct^{n-1}\).
  • Area under a velocity-time graph = displacement over that interval.

Class Day 6: Kinematic Equations for Constant Acceleration

Why this matters

So far we’ve talked about velocity and acceleration in fairly general terms. Today we specialize to the single most common and useful case in this course: motion where acceleration is constant — not changing at all over the interval in question. Under that one assumption, four algebraic equations let you solve almost any 1D motion problem without any calculus at all.

The four equations

Each of the four kinematic equations connects a different subset of five key variables: initial velocity \(v_0\), final velocity (v), acceleration (a), time (t), and displacement (x) (measured from some starting position \(x_0\)). Crucially, each equation is missing exactly one of these five variables — which is precisely why you have four different tools to pick from, depending on what you’re given and what you’re asked to find.

\[v = v_0 + at \qquad\qquad (\text{missing } x)\] \[x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2 \qquad\qquad (\text{missing } v)\] \[v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x-x_0) \qquad\qquad (\text{missing } t)\] \[x = x_0 + \tfrac12(v_0+v)t \qquad\qquad (\text{missing } a)\]

The real skill: choosing the right equation

The formulas themselves are simple to write down; the actual skill this unit is testing is a bookkeeping habit. Before touching any algebra, make a habit of listing out, explicitly:

  1. What variables am I given?
  2. What variable am I asked to find?
  3. Which of the five variables (v₀, v, a, t, x) is not mentioned at all?

Whichever equation omits that missing variable is the one to use — you’ll never need a variable you don’t have, and you won’t be missing anything you need.

Worked example: A car traveling at 18 m/s brakes at a constant deceleration and stops after 60 m. Find the deceleration and the stopping time.

Known: \(v_0=18\), (v=0) (stops), \(x-x_0=60\). Missing: (t). So use the equation that omits (t):

\[v^2=v_0^2+2a(x-x_0) \Rightarrow 0 = 18^2+2a(60) \Rightarrow a = \frac{-324}{120} = -2.7\text{ m/s}^2\]

Now that we have (a), find (t) from the simplest remaining equation, \(v=v_0+at\):

\[0 = 18+(-2.7)t \Rightarrow t = 6.67\text{ s}\]

Notice the two-step structure: often you’ll need one equation to find an intermediate unknown (here, (a)), then a second equation to reach the final answer. This is completely normal and not a sign you picked the wrong approach.

The constant-acceleration assumption is not optional

All four equations are built on the assumption that acceleration doesn’t change during the interval in question. If a problem describes acceleration that changes partway through (a car that speeds up, then cruises, then brakes), you cannot apply one of these equations across the entire trip — you must break the motion into separate constant-acceleration segments and analyze each one, using the final conditions of one segment as the starting conditions of the next.

Common mistake to watch for

A frequent error is quietly assuming an object “must” still be moving (or must have already stopped) without checking. If a quadratic equation from these formulas gives you two mathematical solutions, always ask which one is physically consistent with the situation described — for instance, checking whether the implied stopping time is actually reachable within the given time window, since one algebraic root sometimes describes a scenario that contradicts the problem’s own setup.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • \(v=v_0+at\) (no x); \(x=x_0+v_0t+\tfrac12at^2\) (no v); \(v^2=v_0^2+2a(x-x_0)\) (no t); \(x=x_0+(v_0+v)t\) (no a).
  • Identify the missing variable first, then pick the matching equation.
  • These equations require constant acceleration; segment the motion if acceleration changes.

Class Day 7: Free Fall

Why this matters

Free fall is not a new topic — it’s the kinematic equations from yesterday, applied to the single most common constant acceleration in everyday life: gravity. Once you accept that acceleration due to gravity is constant (approximately \(9.8\text{ m/s}^2\) near Earth’s surface, ignoring air resistance), everything from Day 3 applies directly.

Setting up the sign convention

The one new wrinkle in free-fall problems is that you must choose a sign convention for “up” and “down” before writing any equation, and then stick with it rigidly. The most common choice: up is positive. Under that choice, the acceleration due to gravity is:

\[a = -g = -9.8\text{ m/s}^2\]

— always negative, because gravity always pulls downward, regardless of whether the object is moving up, down, or momentarily still. This single decision, applied consistently, resolves the overwhelming majority of sign errors students make in this unit. If you instead choose down as positive, gravity becomes (+9.8), but every other velocity and position value in the problem must also flip sign accordingly — the important thing is internal consistency, not which convention you pick.

The three canonical scenarios

Dropped from rest (\(v_0=0\)): the object simply falls, gaining speed at a constant rate. (v = -gt), \(y = y_0 - \tfrac12gt^2\) (taking up as positive, so (y) decreases).

Thrown downward (\(v_0 < 0\), moving down from the start): same equations, just with a nonzero negative starting velocity.

Thrown upward (\(v_0 > 0\)): this is the richest case, and the one most worth dwelling on. The object decelerates on the way up (gravity opposes the upward motion), momentarily has zero velocity at the peak, then accelerates back downward — but the acceleration is (-g) the entire time, never changing, never pausing, not even at the exact instant velocity is zero.

Worked example: A ball is thrown straight up at 18 m/s. Find the maximum height and the total time in the air (until it returns to the launch height).

At maximum height, (v=0). Using \(v^2=v_0^2+2a(y-y_0)\):

\[0 = 18^2 + 2(-9.8)(h) \Rightarrow h = \frac{324}{19.6} = 16.5\text{ m}\]

For total time in the air, use the symmetry of the motion: the time to go up equals the time to come back down (as long as launch and landing heights are the same), so find the time to reach the peak using \(v=v_0+at\):

\[0 = 18+(-9.8)t_{up} \Rightarrow t_{up}=1.84\text{ s} \Rightarrow t_{total}=2\times1.84=3.67\text{ s}\]

The most important conceptual point of the whole day

At the very peak of an upward throw, velocity is exactly zero — but acceleration is not. This is the detail students most often get wrong, usually by assuming “zero velocity” implies “zero everything.” Gravity never stops acting on the ball, even for that single instant; if it did, the ball would simply hover there forever, which obviously doesn’t happen. The velocity is zero at that instant precisely because a constant downward acceleration has been continuously eating away at the initial upward velocity the whole time, and is about to keep going and push the velocity negative.

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting to double-check whether a problem intends “height above the ground” or “height above the launch point” — these are different reference points whenever the object is thrown from an elevated platform rather than ground level, and mixing them up is one of the most common sources of an answer that’s off by exactly the platform height.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • \(g\approx9.8\text{ m/s}^2\), directed downward; with up as positive, (a=-g).
  • All four kinematic equations from Day 3 apply directly, with (a=-g).
  • At the peak of upward motion, (v=0) but (a=-g) still, since gravity never stops acting.
  • Time up equals time down when launch and landing heights match (by symmetry).

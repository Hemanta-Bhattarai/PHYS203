Chapter 11: Sound

Class Day 33: Production, Propagation, and Speed of Sound

Why this matters

Sound is the most immediately familiar wave phenomenon in everyday life, and it serves as a natural capstone to the wave chapter: it’s a real, physical longitudinal wave, and its speed depends in a very tangible way on the medium it’s traveling through.

How sound is produced and propagates

A vibrating source (a guitar string, a speaker cone, vocal cords) pushes and pulls on the surrounding medium, creating a traveling pattern of compressions (regions of higher pressure/density) and rarefactions (regions of lower pressure/density) that propagates outward — exactly the longitudinal wave behavior introduced on Day 2 of the waves chapter. Because sound relies entirely on pushing and pulling actual matter, it cannot travel through a vacuum — unlike light, which requires no medium at all. This is why, despite what movies often depict, an explosion in the vacuum of space would be silent to a nearby (unprotected) observer.

Speed of sound depends on the medium

Sound generally travels fastest through solids (tightly bonded, closely packed molecules transmit the pressure disturbance efficiently), slower through liquids, and slowest through gases (loosely spaced molecules take longer to pass the disturbance along). In air specifically, speed of sound also depends on temperature, since warmer air molecules move faster and transmit the pressure wave more quickly:

\[v \approx 331 + 0.6T \quad (T \text{ in } °C)\]

Worked example: Find the speed of sound in air at 15°C.

\[v = 331+0.6(15) = 331+9 = 340\text{ m/s}\]

This 340 m/s value (at a comfortable room-ish temperature) is worth memorizing roughly, since it recurs constantly throughout this chapter’s problems.

Common mistake to watch for

Treating the speed of sound as a fixed universal constant (the way the speed of light in a vacuum is). It is very much not constant — it changes noticeably with temperature in air, and dramatically between different media (roughly four times faster in water than in air, despite water being denser, because water’s much greater stiffness — its resistance to compression — more than compensates for its higher density in determining wave speed).

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave requiring a medium; it cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Speed of sound: generally fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases.
  • In air: (v+0.6T) (T in °C) — roughly 340 m/s at typical room temperature.

Class Day 34: Sound Intensity and Decibels

Why this matters

Human hearing spans an enormous range of actual physical intensities — from the faintest audible whisper to a painfully loud concert, a ratio of roughly a trillion to one. The decibel scale exists specifically to make this vast range manageable with ordinary-sized numbers.

Sound intensity

Intensity is defined as power spread over an area:

\[I = \frac{P}{A}\]

measured in W/m². For a source radiating outward in all directions, this same total power spreads over an ever-larger sphere as you move farther away, which is why intensity decreases (specifically, following an inverse-square relationship) with distance from a point source.

The decibel scale

Because the range of intensities the ear can detect is so enormous, sound level is instead expressed on a logarithmic scale, in decibels:

\[\beta = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)\]

where \(I_0=1\times10^{-12}\) is a standard reference intensity (roughly the quietest sound a healthy human ear can detect). The logarithm compresses that trillion-to-one range of actual intensities down into a much more manageable 0–120ish decibel range for everyday sounds.

The single most important practical consequence of the logarithmic scale, worth internalizing directly: a 10 dB increase corresponds to a 10-times increase in actual intensity — not a 10% increase, not a doubling, a full order of magnitude. Correspondingly, doubling the actual intensity only raises the decibel level by about 3 dB (since (10_{10}(2))).

Worked example: A sound intensity increases from \(1\times10^{-5}\text{ W/m}^2\) to \(1\times10^{-3}\text{ W/m}^2\). Find the change in decibel level.

The intensity ratio is \(10^{-3}/10^{-5}=100\), and \(\beta = 10\log_{10}(100)=10(2)=20\) dB — so this change in decibel level is +20 dB, corresponding to the intensity increasing by a factor of exactly 100.

Common mistake to watch for

Treating decibels as a linear scale — for instance, assuming that 100 dB is “twice as loud” as 50 dB simply because 100 is double 50. Because the scale is logarithmic, a 50 dB difference actually corresponds to an intensity ratio of \(10^5\) (a hundred-thousand-fold difference in actual physical intensity), not a factor of 2. Perceived loudness itself is a separate, more complicated psychological/physiological question beyond just the raw decibel number, but the physical intensity ratio absolutely does not scale linearly with the decibel number.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Sound intensity: (I=P/A) (W/m²), decreasing with distance from a source as power spreads over a larger area.
  • Decibel level: (_{10}\(I/I_0\)), with \(I_0=1\times10^{-12}\).
  • A 10 dB increase = 10× the actual intensity; doubling intensity raises the level by about 3 dB. The scale is logarithmic, not linear.

Class Day 35: The Doppler Effect

Why this matters

This final topic of the semester explains a phenomenon nearly everyone has experienced directly: the pitch of a passing ambulance siren or car horn noticeably drops as the vehicle passes and moves away. The Doppler effect is the physical explanation, and it’s a satisfying, concrete note to end the course’s content on.

The underlying mechanism

As a sound source moves toward a stationary observer, each successive wave crest is emitted from a slightly closer position than the last, effectively “bunching up” the wavefronts ahead of the source — this compresses the effective wavelength reaching the observer, and since (v=f) with (v) fixed by the medium, a smaller wavelength means a higher observed frequency (a higher-pitched sound). As the source moves away, the opposite happens: wavefronts spread out behind it, effectively increasing the wavelength reaching a trailing observer, and producing a lower observed frequency.

The same logic works in reverse for a moving observer approaching or receding from a stationary source: an approaching observer effectively “runs into” wavefronts more frequently than a stationary observer would, raising the observed frequency; a receding observer encounters them less frequently, lowering it.

Formulas (careful with signs)

The general Doppler formula, with (v) the speed of sound, \(v_s\) the source’s speed, and \(v_o\) the observer’s speed:

\[f_{observed} = f_{source}\left(\frac{v\pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}\right)\]

with the sign chosen based on whether the motion brings source and observer closer together (use the combination that increases the observed frequency) or farther apart (use the combination that decreases it). Rather than memorizing sign rules abstractly, it’s more reliable to reason physically for each specific case: “is this motion making the frequency higher or lower?” and choose the sign accordingly.

Worked example: An ambulance siren emits 750 Hz while approaching a stationary observer at 25 m/s (speed of sound 343 m/s).

Approaching source raises the observed frequency, so we use the version of the formula that increases it (denominator decreases, since the source is “catching up” to its own wavefronts):

\[f_{obs} = 750\left(\frac{343}{343-25}\right) = 750\left(\frac{343}{318}\right) = 809.4\text{ Hz}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Believing the Doppler effect changes the actual frequency the source is emitting. It does not — the source itself continues emitting at its own true, unchanged frequency the entire time; only the frequency an outside observer perceives is shifted, due to the changing spatial relationship between successive wavefronts and that particular observer’s position.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Approaching source or approaching observer: observed frequency increases. Receding source or receding observer: observed frequency decreases.
  • General formula: (f_{obs}=f_{source}\(\left(\frac{v\pm v_o}{v\mp v_s}\right)\)) — choose signs by reasoning physically about whether the relative motion should raise or lower the observed frequency.
  • The Doppler effect changes only the perceived frequency at the observer’s location — the source’s true emitted frequency never changes.

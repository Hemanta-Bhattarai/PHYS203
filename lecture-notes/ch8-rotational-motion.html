Chapter 8: Rotational Motion

Class Day 22: Angular Kinematics

Why this matters

Rotational motion turns out to be a near-perfect mirror of everything you already know from Chapter 2 — the same equations, the same logic, just describing angles instead of positions. Today establishes that mapping explicitly, so the rest of the chapter feels like familiar territory wearing new notation.

The direct analogy

Angular position \(\theta\), angular velocity (=d/dt), and angular acceleration (=d/dt) play exactly the roles that (x), (v), and (a) played in Chapter 2. In fact, the rotational kinematic equations are literally the same four equations from Chapter 2, Day 3, with the substitutions (x), (v), (a):

\[\omega = \omega_0+\alpha t \qquad \theta=\theta_0+\omega_0t+\tfrac12\alpha t^2 \qquad \omega^2=\omega_0^2+2\alpha(\theta-\theta_0)\]

Every technique from that day — identifying the missing variable, choosing the matching equation — transfers over unchanged.

Connecting angular and linear quantities

The one genuinely new piece is the bridge between angular quantities (which describe the whole rigid object, regardless of size) and linear quantities at a specific point on that object (which depend on how far that point is from the rotation axis):

\[v = r\omega \qquad a_t = r\alpha\]

Every point on a rigid rotating object shares the same \(\omega\) and \(\alpha\) — but points farther from the axis sweep out more linear distance per rotation, so they have a larger linear speed (v), even though the whole object is “rotating at the same rate.” This resolves the seemingly odd fact that a horse near the outer rim of a merry-go-round moves faster (in m/s) than a horse near the center, even though both complete each full circle in exactly the same time.

Worked example: A fan blade rotates at 15 rad/s. Find the linear speed of a point 0.2 m from the center.

\[v = r\omega = 0.2(15) = 3\text{ m/s}\]

Unit conversions you’ll use constantly

Angular velocity is often given in revolutions per minute (rpm) rather than rad/s, so converting between them is a routine first step:

\[\omega\,[\text{rad/s}] = \omega\,[\text{rpm}]\times\frac{2\pi\text{ rad}}{1\text{ rev}}\times\frac{1\text{ min}}{60\text{ s}}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Confusing “the wheel is spinning at a constant rate” (constant \(\omega\)) with “every point on the wheel moves at the same speed” — these are not the same claim. \(\omega\) is shared by every point on a rigid body; (v=r) is not, since it depends on (r), which differs from point to point.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Rotational kinematics mirrors linear kinematics exactly: (x), (v), (a), same four equations.
  • (v=r), \(a_t=r\alpha\): all points on a rigid body share \(\omega\) and \(\alpha\), but linear speed/acceleration depend on radius.
  • Convert rpm to rad/s via (/60).

Class Day 23: Rotational Kinetic Energy, Moment of Inertia, and Conservation of Energy in Rotational Motion

Why this matters

Just as linear motion has kinetic energy, rotating objects carry their own form of kinetic energy — and the quantity that plays the role of “mass” in that formula, moment of inertia, turns out to depend on more than just how much mass an object has.

Moment of inertia: mass isn’t the whole story

Rotational kinetic energy is:

\[KE_{rot} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\]

directly paralleling \(\tfrac12mv^2\), with moment of inertia (I) playing the role of mass. But unlike ordinary mass, (I) depends critically on how that mass is distributed relative to the rotation axis — the same total mass arranged farther from the axis produces a larger moment of inertia (harder to spin up or slow down) than the same mass concentrated near the axis. This is why a figure skater spins faster when pulling their arms in (Day 5 will make this precise): they’re not changing their mass, only how far it’s distributed from their spin axis.

Standard formulas for common shapes (all with mass (M), radius (R)):

\[I_{disk}=\tfrac12MR^2 \qquad I_{ring}=MR^2 \qquad I_{sphere}=\tfrac25MR^2 \qquad I_{rod,center}=\tfrac{1}{12}ML^2\]

Notice a ring has twice the moment of inertia of a solid disk of the same mass and radius — all of a ring’s mass sits at the maximum distance (R) from the axis, while a disk’s mass is spread out from the center to the edge, with much of it closer in.

Total kinetic energy and energy conservation with rotation

When an object both moves through space and spins (like a ball rolling down a hill), total kinetic energy includes both contributions:

\[KE_{total} = \tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac12I\omega^2\]

This modifies the conservation-of-energy equation from Chapter 7: some of the gravitational PE converts into rotational KE, not just translational KE, meaning a rolling object reaches the bottom of a hill slower than a frictionlessly sliding (non-rotating) object of the same mass, since rolling “diverts” part of the available energy into spinning rather than pure forward motion.

Worked example: A falling 3 kg mass is connected to a pulley (treated as having moment of inertia \(I=0.05\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\), radius 0.15 m) via a string. Find the mass’s speed after falling 2 m.

Energy conservation, now including the pulley’s rotational KE, with (=v/R) linking the pulley’s spin to the string’s (and mass’s) speed:

\[mgh = \tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12I\omega^2 = \tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12I\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)^2\] \[3(9.8)(2) = \tfrac12(3)v^2+\tfrac12(0.05)\frac{v^2}{0.15^2}\] \[58.8 = 1.5v^2+1.111v^2 = 2.611v^2 \Rightarrow v=\sqrt{22.52}=4.75\text{ m/s}\]

Compare this to what you’d get ignoring the pulley’s mass entirely ((v==6.26) m/s) — the pulley’s own rotational inertia noticeably slows the fall, since some of the gravitational PE has to go into spinning the pulley up, not just accelerating the falling mass.

Common mistake to watch for

Using \(I=mR^2\) automatically for every rotating object, regardless of shape. That formula is only exactly correct for a thin ring (all mass at radius (R)) — using it for a solid disk or sphere significantly overestimates the true moment of inertia and gives incorrect energy-conservation results.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • \(KE_{rot}=\tfrac12I\omega^2\); (I) depends on both mass and how it’s distributed relative to the axis (not just total mass).
  • Standard shapes: disk \(\tfrac12MR^2\); ring \(MR^2\); sphere \(\tfrac25MR^2\); rod about center \(\tfrac1{12}ML^2\).
  • Total KE for rolling/rotating-and-translating objects: \(\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12I\omega^2\) — rotational KE “diverts” energy away from pure translational motion.

Class Day 24: Torque I

Why this matters

Torque is the rotational equivalent of force: just as force causes (linear) acceleration, torque causes angular acceleration. Today builds the definition and intuition; tomorrow connects it to Newton’s second law for rotation.

Defining torque

\[\tau = rF\sin\theta\]

where (r) is the distance from the rotation axis to the point where the force is applied, (F) is the force’s magnitude, and \(F\) is the angle between the position vector (from the axis to the application point) and the force vector itself.

Building intuition with the wrench example

Everyone has an intuitive sense that a longer wrench makes it easier to loosen a stuck bolt — torque makes this precise. For the same applied force, a larger (r) (longer wrench) produces proportionally more torque. Equally important: the angle matters just as much as the length. A force applied exactly along the wrench’s handle (pushing or pulling straight along its length, (°)) produces zero torque, no matter how large that force is, since (°=0) — all of that force is “wasted,” contributing nothing to rotating the bolt. Torque is maximized when the force is applied perpendicular to the wrench ((°), (°=1)), which is exactly why you push perpendicular to a wrench handle, not along it, to loosen a bolt most effectively.

Worked example: A mechanic applies 200 N of force at the end of a 0.6 m breaker bar, but the force is angled 15° from perpendicular to the bar. Find the actual torque generated.

Since the force is 15° from perpendicular, the angle from the bar itself (needed in the formula) is (90°-15°=75°):

\[\tau = rF\sin\theta = 0.6(200)\sin75° = 0.6(200)(0.966) = 115.9\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\]

(Equivalently, you could compute this as (rF(15°)), using the angle from perpendicular directly with cosine instead — both give the same answer, since ((90°-)=).)

The moment-arm alternative view

An equivalent and sometimes more intuitive way to compute torque: find the moment arm, the perpendicular (shortest) distance from the rotation axis to the line along which the force acts (extended infinitely in both directions if needed), then simply multiply (= F()). This gives identical results to the (rF) formula, since the moment arm works out to exactly (r).

Common mistake to watch for

Using the full distance (r) in the torque formula while forgetting the \(\sin\theta\) factor entirely — this only gives the correct torque when the force happens to already be perpendicular to the position vector ((°)). For any other angle, skipping the \(\sin\theta\) factor systematically overestimates the true torque.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Torque: (=rF), the rotational analog of force.
  • A force applied along the position vector ((°)) produces zero torque, regardless of magnitude; torque is maximized when force is perpendicular to the position vector.
  • Equivalent moment-arm view: (= F()).

Class Day 25: Torque II and Newton’s Second Law for Rotation

Why this matters

Today completes the rotational analogy begun on Day 1: just as net force produces linear acceleration via F=ma, net torque produces angular acceleration via an exactly parallel equation.

Newton’s second law for rotation

\[\tau_{net} = I\alpha\]

Moment of inertia (I) plays the same role here that mass played in the linear version — a larger (I) means a given torque produces a smaller angular acceleration, exactly as a larger mass means a given force produces a smaller (linear) acceleration.

The problem-solving process mirrors Chapter 5 exactly: identify every torque acting on the rotating object (being careful about sign — torques that would spin the object in opposite rotational directions must be given opposite signs), sum them to find the net torque, then divide by (I) to get \(\alpha\).

Worked example: A wheel (\(I=8\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\)) has an applied torque of 20 N·m and a frictional opposing torque of 4 N·m. Find the net angular acceleration.

\[\tau_{net} = 20-4 = 16\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} \qquad \alpha = \frac{\tau_{net}}{I} = \frac{16}{8} = 2\text{ rad/s}^2\]

Closing the loop back to Day 1

Once \(\alpha\) is known from \(\tau_{net}=I\alpha\), you’re right back in Day 1’s territory: the same rotational kinematic equations (\(\omega=\omega_0+\alpha t\), etc.) let you find the resulting angular velocity, angular displacement, or time, exactly as before. This is worth seeing explicitly as a complete pipeline: forces/torques (today) determine acceleration, which kinematics (Day 1) then propagates forward in time — the exact same two-stage structure as Chapter 5’s incline problems, just in rotational form.

Worked example (full pipeline): A flywheel (\(I=12\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\)) has a net torque of 24 N·m. Find its angular acceleration, and, if starting from rest, its angular velocity after 5 s.

\[\alpha = \frac{24}{12}=2\text{ rad/s}^2 \qquad \omega = \omega_0+\alpha t = 0+2(5) = 10\text{ rad/s}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting to assign consistent signs to torques that act in opposing rotational directions (clockwise vs. counterclockwise) — exactly analogous to Chapter 5’s warning about signs for opposing linear forces. Pick one rotational direction as positive at the start of a problem and apply it consistently to every torque, or the net-torque sum will be wrong.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Newton’s second law for rotation: ({net}=I), directly paralleling (F{net}=ma).
  • Sum torques with consistent sign convention (clockwise vs. counterclockwise), just as with opposing linear forces.
  • Once \(\alpha\) is found, Day 1’s rotational kinematic equations complete the problem (find \(\omega\), \(\theta\), or (t)).

Class Day 26: Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Why this matters

Angular momentum is the rotational sibling of linear momentum from Chapter 6, and its conservation principle produces one of the most visually striking and memorable results in the whole course: the spinning figure skater speeding up as they pull their arms in.

Defining angular momentum

\[L = I\omega\]

directly paralleling (p=mv). Just as linear momentum is conserved in an isolated system, angular momentum is conserved whenever no external torque acts on a system — this follows from Newton’s third law for rotation, exactly mirroring Chapter 6’s derivation for linear momentum.

The signature result: spin rate changes when moment of inertia changes

Since (L=I) must remain constant (with no external torque), if (I) decreases, \(\omega\) must increase to compensate, and vice versa:

\[I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\]

This is exactly what happens when a figure skater pulls their arms in during a spin: bringing their arms and mass closer to their body’s rotation axis decreases their moment of inertia (recall Day 2: mass distributed closer to the axis means smaller (I)), and since angular momentum must stay the same, their angular velocity shoots up dramatically — the skater spins visibly faster, with no additional torque needed at all.

Worked example: A figure skater’s moment of inertia decreases from 5 kg·m² to 2.5 kg·m² while spinning at 3 rad/s. Find the new angular velocity.

\[I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2 \Rightarrow 5(3) = 2.5\,\omega_2 \Rightarrow \omega_2 = 6\text{ rad/s}\]

Halving the moment of inertia exactly doubles the angular velocity — a clean, direct inverse relationship whenever no external torque is present.

Worth noting: kinetic energy is not conserved here

It’s worth being explicit about a subtlety: while angular momentum is conserved in the skater example, rotational kinetic energy is not — it actually increases as the skater pulls their arms in (you can check this: (KE_{rot}=12I^2=(L)), and since (L) is fixed but \(\omega\) increases, \(KE_{rot}\) increases too). This extra energy comes from the real, physical work the skater’s muscles do pulling their arms inward against the outward-pulling effect of their own spin — angular momentum conservation doesn’t mean all rotational quantities are conserved, only angular momentum itself.

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming that because momentum (linear or angular) is conserved, kinetic energy must be too. As the skater example shows directly, this is false in general — momentum conservation and energy conservation are two separate principles, each requiring their own justification (absence of external torque/force for momentum; absence of non-conservative forces/energy input for energy) in any given problem.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Angular momentum: (L=I), conserved when no external torque acts on the system.
  • Decreasing (I) (arms pulled in) increases \(\omega\) proportionally, and vice versa, following \(I_1\omega_1=I_2\omega_2\).
  • Angular momentum conservation does not imply kinetic energy conservation — these are separate principles.

Class Day 27: Rolling Motion and Gyroscopic Motion

Why this matters

Today closes out the rotational motion unit by combining everything so far — kinematics, energy, and moment of inertia — into the classic “rolling without slipping” scenario, then briefly introduces the striking, somewhat mysterious-seeming behavior of spinning gyroscopes.

The rolling-without-slipping condition

When an object rolls along a surface without sliding, its center-of-mass translational speed and its spin rate are locked together by:

\[v = r\omega\]

This connects Day 1’s linear/angular speed relationship directly to a real physical constraint, rather than just an abstract point-on-a-rotating-object calculation.

The famous race: which shape wins?

Combining (v=r) with Day 2’s total kinetic energy formula and Chapter 7’s energy conservation produces one of the most quotable results in introductory physics. Substituting (=v/R) into \(KE_{total}=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12I\omega^2\), and using \(I=cmR^2\) (where (c) is a shape-dependent constant: \(\tfrac12\) for a disk, \(\tfrac25\) for a sphere, (1) for a ring), gives:

\[mgh = \tfrac12mv^2(1+c) \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+c}}\]

The smaller (c) is, the larger the final speed — meaning objects with mass concentrated closer to their center (lower (c)) win a race down an incline. Ranking by (c): sphere ((c=0.4)) beats disk ((c=0.5)) beats ring ((c=1.0)). Notice this ranking has nothing to do with mass or size at all — a small solid sphere and a large solid sphere reach the bottom at the exact same speed, since (m) and (R) both cancel out of the final formula entirely, leaving only the shape-dependent constant (c).

Gyroscopic motion: a brief conceptual introduction

A rapidly spinning object carries a large angular momentum vector (L=I), pointing along its spin axis. When gravity (or any other torque) tries to tip that spin axis over, something initially counterintuitive happens: instead of simply toppling over the way a non-spinning object would, the spin axis instead sweeps around slowly in a cone-shaped path, a behavior called precession. This happens because a torque changes angular momentum gradually, in the direction the torque points, rather than instantaneously toppling the whole object — and for a rapidly spinning object with a large (L), that same-sized torque produces only a small, slow change in the direction of (L), rather than immediately collapsing it. The faster the spin (larger (L)), the slower and more resistant to tipping the precession becomes — which is precisely why gyroscopes are used for navigation and stabilization: a rapidly spinning gyroscope maintains a very stable orientation in space over the short term, largely resisting outside disturbances.

Common mistake to watch for

Describing a gyroscope as somehow “defying gravity.” It isn’t — gravity (or any applied torque) is still fully acting on it the whole time; the surprising part is how angular momentum responds to that torque (slow precession of direction, rather than the object simply falling over), not that gravity has stopped acting.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Rolling without slipping: (v=r).
  • Race-down-an-incline ranking (fastest to slowest, same mass/radius/height): solid sphere > solid disk > ring/hoop, since a smaller shape-constant (c) in \(I=cmR^2\) diverts less energy into rotation.
  • Gyroscopic precession: a spinning object’s angular momentum vector resists rapid changes in direction, causing slow precession rather than simply toppling, when a torque (like gravity) acts on it.

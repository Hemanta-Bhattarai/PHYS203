Chapter 6: Momentum and Collisions

Class Day 17: Momentum, Impulse, and Conservation of Momentum

Why this matters

Momentum offers a different, often more powerful lens than F=ma for analyzing certain problems — especially collisions, where forces act for very short, hard-to-measure times, but momentum still gives exact, clean answers.

Momentum and impulse

Momentum is defined as (p = mv) — a vector combining mass and velocity into a single measure of “how hard it would be to stop this object.” A heavy truck moving slowly and a light car moving fast can have comparable momentum, even though their speeds are very different.

Impulse is the change in momentum, and it’s directly connected to force through a short derivation from Newton’s second law. Since (F=ma=m(v/t)), rearranging gives:

\[F\Delta t = m\Delta v = \Delta p\]

This is the impulse-momentum theorem: the impulse delivered (force multiplied by the time it acts) equals the resulting change in momentum. The practical, real-world payoff of this equation is enormous: for a fixed impulse (a fixed change in momentum, such as bringing a passenger’s momentum to zero in a crash), stretching out the time (t) over which that change happens directly reduces the average force required, since (F=p/t). This is exactly why airbags, crumple zones, and padded gymnastics mats work: they don’t change the total impulse needed to stop something, they just spread it over more time, lowering the peak force.

Worked example: A 0.5 kg ball is struck by a bat, changing its velocity from −10 m/s to 15 m/s in 0.01 s. Find the impulse and the average force.

\[J = \Delta p = m(v_f-v_i) = 0.5(15-(-10)) = 12.5\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\] \[F_{avg} = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{12.5}{0.01} = 1250\text{ N}\]

Conservation of momentum

For an isolated system (no significant external forces), total momentum is conserved — it’s exactly the same before and after any interaction, such as a collision or explosion. This follows directly from Newton’s third law: internal forces between two colliding objects are always equal and opposite, so they exactly cancel when you add up the total momentum change of the whole system.

\[p_{total,before} = p_{total,after}\]

Worked example: A 4 kg object at rest is struck by a 2 kg object moving at 6 m/s, and they stick together. Find their combined velocity.

Before: total momentum (= 2(6)+4(0) = 12) kg·m/s. After, they move together with combined mass 6 kg:

\[12 = 6v_f \Rightarrow v_f = 2\text{ m/s}\]

Common mistake to watch for

Forgetting that momentum, like velocity, is a signed vector quantity — in a one-dimensional collision, objects moving in opposite directions before impact have momenta with opposite signs, and you must keep the signs consistent throughout, or the conservation equation won’t balance correctly.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Momentum: (p = mv). Impulse: (J = Ft = p).
  • Increasing collision time (same impulse) reduces average force — the physics behind airbags and crumple zones.
  • Total momentum of an isolated system is conserved, a direct consequence of Newton’s third law.

Class Day 18: Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

Why this matters

Not all collisions behave the same way once you look beyond momentum. Today draws a sharp distinction that matters enormously in practice: whether kinetic energy is also conserved, in addition to momentum.

The key distinction

Momentum is conserved in essentially every collision (as long as the system is isolated), but kinetic energy is only conserved in elastic collisions. A collision is called: - Elastic if kinetic energy is conserved (objects bounce apart, none of the mechanical energy converts to heat, sound, or permanent deformation). - Inelastic if kinetic energy is not conserved (some is converted to other forms). A perfectly inelastic collision is the extreme case where the objects stick together and move with a common final velocity.

The clean special case: equal-mass elastic collision

When two objects of equal mass collide elastically, with one initially at rest, a remarkably clean result emerges: they simply exchange velocities. The moving object stops completely, and the previously stationary object moves off with the exact velocity the first one had. This is worth internalizing as a sanity-check case, since it’s easy to verify (both momentum and kinetic energy are trivially conserved by a simple swap) and often shows up as a special case within larger problems.

Worked example: perfectly inelastic collision and energy loss

A 1500 kg car moving at 15 m/s collides with a stationary 1000 kg car, and they lock together. Find their combined velocity and the kinetic energy lost.

Momentum conservation: (1500(15) = (1500+1000)v_f v_f = 9).

Kinetic energy before: ((1500)(15)^2 = 168{,}750) J. Kinetic energy after: ((2500)(9)^2=101{,}250) J.

\[\text{KE lost} = 168{,}750-101{,}250 = 67{,}500\text{ J}\]

This lost energy doesn’t vanish — it converts into the sound of the crash, permanent deformation of the vehicles (crumpling), heat, and so on. This is precisely why cars are engineered to crumple in a collision: dissipating kinetic energy into crushing metal, rather than transmitting it directly into the passengers, is what makes the collision survivable, and it’s directly connected to yesterday’s impulse discussion — crumpling also extends the collision’s duration, further lowering the peak force on occupants.

Common mistake to watch for

Assuming kinetic energy is conserved by default, the way momentum is. Only use kinetic-energy conservation as one of your two equations if the problem explicitly states (or the physical scenario clearly implies, such as billiard balls or idealized elastic springs) that the collision is elastic. For an unspecified “generic” collision, momentum conservation is the one tool you can always reach for; kinetic energy conservation requires justification.

Key formulas and facts for this day

  • Momentum is conserved in all isolated-system collisions; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions.
  • Equal-mass elastic collision (one initially at rest): the two objects exchange velocities.
  • Kinetic energy “lost” in an inelastic collision converts to heat, sound, and deformation — it doesn’t disappear, it changes form.
